# Chapter 12: Titles and Abstracts

**Primary source:** *English for Writing Research Papers* (Wallwork), ch. 11–12

## Core Idea

Titles and abstracts are judged **in isolation** — a reader or reviewer decides whether to read your full paper based on these two elements alone, often without any other context. Every word in a title should earn its place by adding real information; every abstract should answer, in order, why you did the work, what you did, what you found, and what it means — with minimal throat-clearing.

## Frameworks Introduced

### Generating and Tightening a Title
Start by answering three questions: What have I found that will attract attention? What is new, different, and interesting about my findings? What are the 3–5 key words that capture what makes this research unique? Build the title from the answers.

**Cut redundant framing words.** Phrases like *"A study of the factors affecting…"* or *"An investigation into some psychological aspects of…"* front-load the title with words that convey no information:
> Weak: *"A study of the factors affecting the trihydroxyindole procedure for the analysis of deoxyribonucleic acid"*
> Tighter: *"Factors affecting the trihydroxyindole procedure for the analysis of deoxyribonucleic acid"*

Similarly redundant words to watch for: *inquiry, analysis, evaluation, assessment*. **Exception:** words like *"study"* or *"investigation"* can be strategically useful when you want the title to sound less conclusive/definitive than a bare noun-phrase title would — e.g., *"An investigation into the determinants of customer satisfaction"* reads as more tentative and open than the flat *"The determinants of customer satisfaction,"* which sounds like you've settled the matter. Swapping *"the"* for *"some"*, or dropping the article entirely, achieves a similar softening effect.

### The Declarative Title Question
A **declarative title** states your main finding as a complete claim (subject-verb-object), e.g., a title asserting a specific causal or definitive result. This can effectively grab attention and is increasingly common in some fields (medicine, biology) — but it's risky if your actual findings are more tentative than the title implies, since it primes readers to treat the claim as already-settled fact. **Before using a declarative title, check how your target journal's recent issues actually use titles** — some fields and venues welcome them, others (and many reviewers) consider them overreach.

### Question Titles
Framing a title as a question (using an auxiliary like *does, would, can, will*, or a question word like *why, when, what, which*) is another attention-getting option, but avoid one that still carries redundant words — a tight, punchy question title beats a wordy one.

### Two-Part Titles
A colon-separated two-part title (*"Old age: diversity among men and women"*) can combine a broad orienting phrase with a specific focus — useful when a single phrase can't capture both the general topic and its specific angle. Words like *"study"* or *"investigation"* are sometimes retained in the first half of a two-part title even when they'd be cut from a single-part title, since the colon structure changes how the words read.

### Abstract Types
- **Unstructured abstract:** one paragraph, ~100–250 words, briefly summarizing every main section of the paper.
- **Structured abstract:** the same content, divided into labeled short sections (e.g., Background, Methods, Results, Conclusions) — format varies by journal.
- **Extended abstract:** a genuine mini-paper (2–4 pages) with its own Introduction/Methods/Discussion structure, sometimes without a further abstract of its own.
- **Conference abstract:** typically standalone (up to ~500 words), used by organizers to decide whether to accept an oral presentation; may take any of the above forms.

**Always check your target journal's or conference's specific instructions** before choosing a format — abstract conventions vary significantly by venue.

### The Four-Question Abstract Structure
Nearly every abstract, regardless of field, answers these questions **in this order**:
1. **Why** did you carry out this project? (context/problem/gap)
2. **What** did you do, and how?
3. **What** were your results — what's new compared to prior work?
4. **What** are the implications — conclusions and/or recommendations?

**Worked example (engineering/applied science pattern):**
> (1) *Context/problem:* Existing models don't adequately predict battery lifetime under repeated recharging. (2) *What was done:* An analytical model was developed relating recharge frequency, recharge duration, and battery lifespan. (3) *Validity:* The model was validated against experimental and simulation data, showing strong agreement. (4) *Results:* The model outperforms previous analytical approaches. (5) *Implications:* The model can guide the design of longer-lasting batteries.

**A critical calibration point: context-setting (question 1) should never exceed roughly 25% of the total abstract.** A reader — especially a reviewer skimming hundreds of abstracts — wants the new information (what you did and found) as quickly as possible; background they likely already know should be minimal here (save the fuller context for your Introduction).

**Field variation:** most fields follow this four-question order, but some don't map cleanly onto "results" — historians, for instance, may not have discrete "findings" in the empirical-science sense, and papers presenting new instrumentation may foreground the apparatus's capabilities and performance rather than a specific empirical result. Adapt the four-question skeleton to what your field actually reports, rather than forcing an artificial "results" section where none naturally exists.

### When to Write the Abstract
Write a **rough draft early** — it forces you to clarify what belongs in the paper and how to structure it. But **finalize the abstract last**, after the full paper is written, mirroring the actual research process: you know what you found before you can accurately summarize why it matters. The same logic often applies to the Introduction (see Chapter 13).

## Key Concepts & Definitions

- **Declarative title:** a title phrased as a complete factual claim rather than a topic description.
- **Context-setting ratio:** the proportion of an abstract devoted to background rather than new findings — should stay under ~25%.
- **Structured vs. unstructured abstract:** whether the abstract is divided into labeled sub-sections or written as one continuous paragraph.

## Mental Models & Metaphors

- **Title and abstract as a "buy/read" decision point** — Wallwork frames the abstract's job explicitly as helping a reader decide whether to invest time reading the full paper, the same way a book's back-cover copy does.
- **Every title word must "pay rent"** — if a word could be cut without losing information, cut it.

## Common Pitfalls / Anti-patterns

- **Front-loading a title with empty framing words** ("A study of...", "An investigation into...") that could simply be deleted.
- **Using a declarative title for tentative or preliminary findings**, implying more certainty than the paper actually supports.
- **Spending more than a quarter of the abstract on background/context** the target reader likely already knows.
- **Drafting the abstract once, early, and never revisiting it** after the paper's content and framing have evolved during writing.
- **Forcing a results-focused four-question structure onto a field (like history) where the natural reporting shape is different.**

## Key Takeaways

- Generate a title from your key findings and 3–5 defining keywords; cut any word that doesn't add information.
- Choose declarative vs. descriptive vs. question-form titles deliberately, based on how confident your findings are and what's conventional in your target venue.
- Structure abstracts around four questions in order: why, what/how, what results, what implications — and keep background under ~25% of the total.
- Draft the abstract early to clarify structure, but finalize it only after the full paper is complete.
- Always check your specific journal's or conference's abstract-format requirements before finalizing.
