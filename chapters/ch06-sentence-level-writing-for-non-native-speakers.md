# Chapter 6: Sentence-Level Writing and Word Order for Non-Native Speakers

**Primary source:** *English for Writing Research Papers* (Wallwork), ch. 2–3, 7

## Core Idea

English enforces a strict, low-flexibility word order (subject–verb–object–indirect object) that many other languages don't. Writers translating rhetorical or grammatical habits from their first language into English frequently produce grammatically parseable but hard-to-follow sentences — not because of vocabulary errors, but because of **word order and subject-placement choices** that violate English readers' expectations. This chapter is Wallwork's most technical and most ESL-specific material, but the underlying principles (put the subject first, keep subject and verb close, prefer short sentences) improve any writer's prose, native speaker or not.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The Fixed English Word Order
> Subject → Verb → Direct Object → Indirect Object

Example: *"The researchers (S) sent (V) their manuscript (DO) to the journal (IO)."* This order is rarely altered, and other sentence elements (time phrases, frequency phrases) should be inserted without breaking up this core chain — e.g., "*Last week* the researchers sent their manuscript to the journal *for the second time*" keeps S-V-DO-IO intact while adding detail at the edges.

**Cross-linguistic awareness:** writers should explicitly compare how their native language orders information against English. German speakers, for instance, often avoid opening a sentence with the subject (preferring "Your letter has reached us" over "We have received your letter") because German syntax can push the verb to the clause's end — a habit that, carried into English, delays the reader's access to the sentence's core action. Russian speakers may under-use word order altogether for grammatical role-marking (relying on case endings instead), producing English sentences where subject and object could be swapped without the writer noticing the ambiguity. Knowing your own language's default pattern is the first diagnostic step — the "error" is often not a vocabulary or grammar mistake but an imported structural habit.

### Ten Concrete Word-Order Rules
1. **Choose the subject that produces the most concise, active sentence** — "Y elicited X" over "X was elicited by Y" when the choice is available.
2. **Don't make impersonal "it" the sentence's subject.** "It is possible to do this with the new system" → "This can be done with the new system." Prefer modal verbs (*might, should, may, must*) to delay-inducing "it is [adjective] that…" constructions.
3. **Don't use a pronoun before introducing the noun it refers to.** A pronoun should only point *backward* to an already-named noun, never forward, forcing the reader to hold an unresolved reference in mind.
4. **Put the subject before the verb.** ("In the survey participated 350 subjects" → "Three hundred and fifty subjects participated in the survey.") Don't delay the subject behind an introductory clause meant to build suspense — that's a rhetorical move some languages use, but not English scientific convention.
5. **Keep subject and verb close together** — the verb carries essential information, and native English speech patterns keep it near its subject. If several long noun phrases must all attach to one verb, consider restructuring with "There is/are…" constructions or splitting into multiple sentences rather than stacking everything before the verb appears.
6. **Avoid parenthetical insertions between subject and verb** — more than a couple of words wedged in there breaks the reader's train of thought and signals (correctly or not) that the inserted material is less important than it may actually be.
7. **Don't separate a verb from its direct object.** Verbs like *associate X with Y, apply X to Y, attribute X to Y* should keep X immediately after the verb; don't invert to "we can associate, with this tool, P and Q."
8. **Put the direct object before the indirect object**, and don't let the indirect object migrate to the sentence's opening position out of habit from another language's syntax.
9. **Place adverbs deliberately** — most one-word frequency adverbs (*usually, often, rarely*) go immediately before the main verb ("Dying neurons do not *usually* exhibit…").
10. **Watch for ambiguous noun strings and adjective placement** — English allows stacking nouns as modifiers (*"data collection error rate"*) far more than many languages, but overuse creates parsing ambiguity; a relative clause is often clearer than a fourth stacked noun.

### The Sentence-Length Discipline
Average English sentence length has been shrinking for centuries: roughly 45 words in Shakespeare's time, ~29 words 150 years ago, and modern writing guides recommend 15–18 words for general prose. **Wallwork's recommended ceiling for academic writing is ~25 words.** The reasoning isn't stylistic snobbery — it's cognitive: readers should be able to process a sentence *once*, at normal reading speed, understanding the logic as they go rather than having to reread it to reconstruct meaning at the end.

**Diagnostic case:** a peer reviewer told an author to "delete this sentence or rewrite so it means something sensible" — the sentence in question ran to roughly 70 words and packed together rarity, unpredictability, diagnostic risk, and a recommendation, all in one breath. The fix wasn't better vocabulary; it was **splitting one overloaded sentence into four shorter ones**, each carrying a single idea, connected by ordinary transitions (*Its behavior... Moreover... It would thus...*).

### Clarifying Who Did What: Active/Passive and the "We" Question
Whether to use first-person "we" or the impersonal passive is often dictated by field convention (physics journals, for instance, frequently prohibit "we," believing science should read as independent of the person reporting it) — **check your target journal's author guidelines before drafting**, since retrofitting a whole draft from one voice to the other is expensive.

If passive voice is required or preferred:
- The passive is genuinely useful for describing *process* (e.g., Methods sections), because it puts the equipment/procedure in the sentence's first position where readers expect the subject: *"Two tests were performed"* rather than *"We performed two tests"* when the tests, not the "we," are what matters to the reader at that point.
- When reporting others' findings passively, prefer fronting the actual subject over a delayed "it": *"Bilingual children have been demonstrated to adapt better…"* rather than *"It has been demonstrated that bilingual children adapt better…"* — the first keeps the subject at the head of the sentence where English readers expect it.
- **Never substitute "someone," "one," or "people"** for a specific unnamed agent in formal scientific writing — these read as informal or vague in this register.
- Whatever voice you choose, the single non-negotiable requirement is that **the reader must always be able to tell your work apart from others' work.** You know which claims are yours; the reader does not, unless tense, voice, and explicit attribution make it unambiguous (see Chapter 7 discussion of "clarifying who did what" via tense choice).

## Key Concepts & Definitions

- **S-V-DO-IO order:** the near-inviolable backbone of an English sentence.
- **Backward vs. forward pronoun reference:** pronouns should point to nouns already introduced, never nouns yet to come.
- **Sentence-length ceiling:** ~25 words as a practical academic-writing target, not an absolute rule but a strong default.
- **Reader-responsibility interference:** word-order habits imported from a reader-responsibility language (see Chapter 5) often manifest specifically as delayed subjects and buried verbs.

## Mental Models & Metaphors

- **The reader as someone reading once, at speed, under time pressure** — every rule in this chapter optimizes for a single, forward pass through the sentence, not a careful multi-pass parse.
- **Subject and verb as a load-bearing pair** that should never be separated by more than a phrase or two — everything else in the sentence is scaffolding around that pair, not a wedge between it.

## Common Pitfalls / Anti-patterns

- **Opening with "It is [adjective] that…"** as a reflexive habit, burying the real subject.
- **Using a pronoun (it, this, these) before the noun it refers to has appeared in the text** — forces the reader to hold an open reference.
- **Delaying the subject behind a long introductory subordinate clause** built for suspense — a technique that can work in narrative prose but reads as evasive or disorganized in scientific writing.
- **Writing 60–80 word sentences that pack multiple independent ideas together** — the single most common reason non-native-English papers get flagged by reviewers for "unclear writing," even when every individual clause is grammatical.
- **Switching between "we" and impersonal passive inconsistently** within one paper without a clear rule for when each applies.
- **Using "someone," "one," or "people"** in place of a specific unnamed agent — reads as informal.

## Key Takeaways

- Default to Subject–Verb–Direct-Object–Indirect-Object order, and audit your own native-language habits for where you might be importing a different pattern.
- Cap most sentences around 25 words; when a sentence runs long, look for a place to split it into two, each carrying one idea.
- Never let a pronoun refer forward to a noun that hasn't appeared yet.
- Check your target journal's style guide for first-person vs. passive before you draft, and stay consistent.
- However you handle voice, always make it unambiguous to the reader which claims are yours and which belong to cited sources.
