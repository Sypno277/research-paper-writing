# Chapter 1: Finding Your Research Question

**Primary sources:** *The Craft of Research* (Booth, Colomb, Williams, Bizup & FitzGerald), ch. 3–4; *A Manual for Writers* (Turabian), ch. 1–2; *Schaum's Quick Guide* (Rozakis), ch. 1–4

## Core Idea

A research project does not begin with a topic — it begins with a **question whose answer matters to someone besides you**. Most struggling papers fail not because the writer didn't gather enough information, but because they never converted a broad interest into a focused, answerable question with a stake attached. The whole first stage of research is a funnel: **interest → topic → focused topic → question → problem**, and at every stage of that funnel you test yourself with one relentless follow-up: *So what?*

## Frameworks Introduced

### The Topic-Narrowing Funnel (Booth et al.)
1. **Subject** — a broad field (e.g., "climate change").
2. **Topic** — a specific interest within that field (e.g., "the effect of climate change on migratory birds").
3. **Focused topic** — narrowed further using an *action word* (a noun derived from a verb: *conflict, contribute, describe, develop*) rather than a static noun. Compare:
   - Too broad: "Free will in Tolstoy" (4–5 words = warning sign)
   - Focused: "The conflict of free will and inevitability in Tolstoy's description of three battles in *War and Peace*"

A useful diagnostic: restate the topic as a full sentence. If the sentence says almost nothing new ("Commercial aviation has a history"), the topic is still too static — it lacks an action word that could drive an argument.

### The Three-Step "So What?" Test (Booth et al. / Turabian)
Once you have a focused topic, build it into a question, then justify the question, in three explicit steps:

1. **Name your topic:** "I am studying/working on ___."
2. **Add an indirect question:** "...because I want to find out who/what/when/where/whether/why/how ___."
3. **Answer *So what?* by motivating the question:** "...in order to help my reader understand ___."

Example chain:
> I am studying the causes of the disappearance of large North American mammals, because I want to find out whether early peoples hunted them to extinction, in order to help my reader understand whether native peoples lived in harmony with nature or helped destroy it.

This three-part sentence is worth writing out literally, even in scratch form, at the start of a project — and revisiting it as the project evolves. Advanced researchers often cannot fill in step 3 confidently until after a first draft; that's normal, but you should never *finish* a project without an answer to it.

### Question vs. Problem
A **question** is not automatically a **problem**. A question "raises a problem" only if *not answering it* costs the reader something they already care about. "Was Lincoln's right thumb longer than his nose?" is a question with no cost to leaving it unanswered — it is not a problem. "Are there ultimate particles?" raises a problem because not answering it blocks something bigger: understanding the nature of physical existence. (Chapter 3 develops this into the full concept of a "research problem.")

### Turabian's Working Hypothesis
Turabian frames the same funnel slightly differently for practical drafting purposes: once you have a question, propose a **working hypothesis** — a tentative answer you don't yet believe with confidence, but which gives you something concrete to test against your sources. If you can't find or defend an answer, you can still write a paper that *argues for the importance of the question itself* (a legitimate research report in fields where no clean answer exists yet).

## Key Concepts & Definitions

- **Subject vs. Topic:** a subject is a field; a topic is a specific angle on it that implies a question.
- **Action word:** a noun built from a verb (*contribution, conflict, effect, development*) that gives a topic direction rather than leaving it inert.
- **So what? test:** the question of why an answer matters to a reader, not just to the researcher.
- **Working hypothesis** (Turabian): a provisional answer used to organize early research, always open to revision or rejection.

## Mental Models & Metaphors

- **The itch metaphor:** research often starts as "a mental itch about a small question that only a single researcher wants to scratch." Trivial-seeming curiosity (why does a coffee spill dry in a ring?) can lead to significant findings — you can't know until you follow it. Don't discard small questions prematurely; test them with *So what?* rather than dismissing them for seeming minor.
- **Topic as "a place" (topos):** narrowing a topic is not shrinking it arbitrarily — it's choosing a specific starting place from which to head in a direction.

## Common Pitfalls / Anti-patterns

- **The "mound of facts" paper.** Beginners often skip straight from a topic to exhaustive note-taking, then dump everything into a paper that concludes "Thus we see many differences and similarities between…" Without a driving question, there is no principle for *selecting* which facts matter.
- **Topics stated in 4–5 words.** ("Free will in Tolstoy," "the history of commercial aviation") are almost always too broad — a warning sign, not a rule, but a reliable one.
- **Narrowing too far.** The opposite failure: choosing a topic so specific that no sources exist on it. Booth's example: too much exists on "the history of commercial aviation" but too little (for a beginner) on "the decision to lengthen the wingtips on the DC-3 prototype for military use." Balance is required.
- **Settling for "just curious."** A fine starting motive, but it will not satisfy a reader forever. If you cannot eventually answer *So what?* for someone besides yourself, the project needs reframing.
- **Questions with dead-end answers.** Test candidate questions against three failure modes: (1) the answer is a settled fact you could just look up; (2) the answer would be purely speculative, with no way to gather evidence; (3) the answer is a genuine dead end that connects to nothing larger.

## Worked Example: Narrowing a Topic

| Stage | Example |
|---|---|
| Subject (too broad) | Masks |
| Topic | Masks in Native American ceremonies |
| Broad questions generated | How did masks originate? What roles do masks play in Hopi dances? How do Native American masks differ from Japanese masks? What if masks became common at other holidays? |
| Combined question | How and why have users of a specific story given a symbolic object mythic quality over time? |
| Three-step "So what?" | *I am studying* how storytellers have used the Alamo legend across generations, *because I want to find out* how and why they gave the event a mythic quality, *in order to help my reader understand* how political interests shape the way national stories get told. |

Booth et al.'s technique for generating candidate questions systematically: ask about your topic's **history** (what came before/after it, how has it changed), its **structure/composition** (how do its parts function within a larger system), its **categories** (how does it group into kinds, how does it compare/contrast with similar things), and then **invert** these into negative and speculative forms ("What if X never existed?"). This six-angle sweep reliably produces more candidate questions than free-associating alone.

## Key Takeaways

- Don't start collecting sources until you have at least a *candidate* question, not just a topic — the question is what tells you which sources matter.
- Every research question should survive the three-step "So what?" test before you commit real time to it.
- A topic phrased as a static noun phrase is a warning sign; rebuild it around an action word.
- Treat "So what?" as a standing question to revisit throughout the project, not a box to check once at the start.
- Small, odd, seemingly trivial questions are legitimate starting points — evaluate them by where they might lead, not by how important they sound on day one.
