# Chapter 5: Organizing the Paper and Paragraphs

**Primary sources:** *The Craft of Research*, ch. 13; *A Manual for Writers* (Turabian), ch. 9; *English for Writing Research Papers* (Wallwork), ch. 4

## Core Idea

Once a draft exists, revision should proceed **top-down**: overall structure first, then sections, then paragraphs, then sentences, then spelling/punctuation last. Readers don't process a paper by adding up sentences like beads on a string — they want a sense of the whole first, and use that sense to interpret the parts. Revising bottom-up wastes effort polishing passages you'll later cut or move.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The Top-Down Revision Sequence (Booth et al.)
1. **Frame** — is it unambiguous where the introduction ends and the conclusion begins? Is the main point stated clearly in (or near) both?
2. **Argument substance** — within each section, is there a workable balance of reasons to evidence?
3. **Argument quality** — is the evidence reliable, are claims appropriately qualified, does it read as a conversation with a skeptical-but-friendly peer, are warrants supplied where needed?
4. **Organization** — do key terms run through the whole paper and each section? Are section boundaries clearly signaled? Does each section's opening explain its relation to what came before?
5. **Paragraphs** — do individual paragraphs lead readers through the conversation coherently?
6. **Sentences, spelling, punctuation** — last, because global cuts and rearrangements make fine-tuning at this level wasted work if done too early.

### Revising the Frame: Three Checks
1. **Add visual separation** (extra space or a heading) so readers can't miss where the introduction ends and the conclusion begins.
2. **State the main point near the end of the introduction, and compare it against the point in the conclusion.** They should not contradict each other — but they also should not be *identical*. The conclusion's version should be more specific and more contestable than the introduction's.
3. **Put key recurring terms in the point sentence of the introduction**, whether that sentence states the full claim or is only a launching point (see Chapter 4).

**Diagnostic worked example:** an introduction ending with "In this paper I will discuss the reasons for the Crusades" announces a topic, not a point — there's no claim there at all. If the conclusion reveals the real point ("But their efforts…apart" — i.e., the Crusades were driven by internal European political concerns as much as religious ones), that point (or a version of it) belongs at the end of the introduction too, not just buried in the conclusion.

### Revising the Argument: Substance and Quality Checks
**Substance test:** identify everything in a section that counts as evidence (summaries, paraphrases, quotations, data, figures). If evidence and its explanation make up less than roughly a third of a section, you may not have enough evidence to support your reasons. If there's abundant evidence but few or no reasons connecting it to your claim, you likely have a "data dump."

**Quality checklist:**
- Is the evidence reliable — accurate, precise, sufficient, representative, authoritative? Check quotations and data against your original notes.
- Have you qualified overreaching claims with appropriate hedges (*probably, most, often, may*)?
- Does the argument read as a conversation with a skeptical-but-friendly peer, acknowledging objections rather than reading like a one-sided contest?
- Have you stated every warrant readers will need but won't already accept? (Write each section's unstated warrant in the margin and ask honestly whether readers will grant it without being told.)

### Revising Organization: The Key-Terms Test
This is the single most concrete diagnostic tool in the chapter:
1. **Circle the key terms** in your main-point sentence (introduction and conclusion).
2. **Circle those same terms** everywhere they recur in the body.
3. If most paragraphs contain none of those circled terms, readers will feel the paper is wandering — either work the terms back in, or recognize that the passage has drifted off-argument and needs rewriting or cutting.
4. Repeat the exercise **per section**: each section needs its *own* distinguishing key terms beyond the whole-paper ones. If a section's "unique" terms turn out to just repeat another section's, consider merging the two — they may be making the same point twice.

Complementary structural checks: can you insert a heading at each major section start without hesitation? Does each section's opening signal its logical relationship to the one before it (*"More important…", "One complication is…", "First… Second…"*)? Can you state, for each section, which of the five argument questions (Chapter 3) it answers — and if it doesn't answer one of those, does it at least supply necessary context? If you can't answer that question, consider cutting the section.

### Paragraph Structure (Wallwork, converging with Booth et al.)
Wallwork frames each paragraph as **a microcosm of the whole paper**: a topic sentence functions like a title, the middle sentences develop it like sections do, and the closing sentence functions like a conclusion. Standard shape for a body paragraph (not the section's opening paragraph):

1. **Topic sentence** — states what the paragraph is about *and* connects to the previous paragraph.
2. **1–8 sentences** developing that one idea in logical sequence.
3. **Concluding sentence** — refers back to the opening or forward to the next paragraph.

One discipline worth internalizing: **one specific idea per sentence, one general idea per paragraph.** If your opening sentence doesn't state the paragraph's point, make sure your closing sentence does — never bury the point in the middle.

**Paragraph length as diagnosis, not rule:** paragraphs vary by genre (shorter in brief reports, longer in critical essays), but strings of very short paragraphs often signal underdeveloped points, while paragraphs running past a page often signal digression. Vary length deliberately — a short paragraph can be an effective way to isolate and emphasize a single strong statement.

### Reader-Responsibility vs. Writer-Responsibility (Wallwork)
A cross-cultural framing worth knowing explicitly, especially for non-native English writers: in many rhetorical traditions, **the reader is expected to do the work** of connecting ideas across sentences and paragraphs ("reader-responsibility"). In English scientific/technical writing, the convention is reversed: **the writer is nearly 100% responsible** for making connections explicit, so the reader can absorb the argument with minimal effort. This isn't a matter of intelligence or politeness — it's a genre convention, and papers that assume reader-responsibility (common among writers translating rhetorical habits from other traditions) read as unclear or poorly organized to English-language reviewers, even when the underlying ideas are sound.

## Key Concepts & Definitions

- **Top-down revision:** structure → sections → paragraphs → sentences → mechanics, in that order.
- **Point sentence:** the sentence in an introduction or conclusion that states the paper's actual claim (as opposed to merely announcing its topic).
- **Key-terms test:** circling recurring terms to verify a paper's coherence is visible on the page, not just in the writer's head.
- **Data dump:** a section with abundant evidence but no reasons connecting it to the claim.
- **Reader-responsibility vs. writer-responsibility:** whether the burden of connecting ideas falls on the reader (many non-English rhetorical traditions) or the writer (English scientific convention).

## Mental Models & Metaphors

- **Reading as building a "sense of the whole" first**, then interpreting parts through that lens — which is *why* top-down revision matches how readers actually process text.
- **The paragraph as a paper in miniature** — topic sentence as title, body as sections, closing sentence as conclusion.
- **Paragraph breaks as conversational pauses** — used to let a reader rest after a strong point, absorb a complex passage, or signal a shift to a new idea, the way pauses function in speech.

## Common Pitfalls / Anti-patterns

- **An introduction that announces a topic instead of stating a point.** ("In this paper I will discuss the reasons for the Crusades" — says nothing contestable.)
- **A conclusion's point that's identical to the introduction's**, adding nothing on a second read — the conclusion's version should sharpen, not just repeat, the claim.
- **Sections with abundant evidence but no explicit reasoning connecting it to the claim** (data dump).
- **Failing to run the key-terms test** and only discovering wandering passages when a reader complains, rather than catching it during self-revision.
- **Assuming reader-responsibility conventions transfer to English scientific writing** — a common, largely invisible-to-the-writer source of "hard to follow" feedback from reviewers.
- **Over-applying "always start with a topic sentence" as a rigid rule.** It's a good default, not an absolute — over-rigid application can make prose feel stiff. What matters is that the point is never buried in the middle.

## Key Takeaways

- Revise top-down: frame, then argument substance/quality, then organization, then paragraphs, then sentences/mechanics — never the reverse.
- Physically mark where your introduction ends and conclusion begins, and check the two point-sentences aren't identical.
- Use the key-terms circling test on your whole draft and on each section individually — it's a concrete, repeatable diagnostic, not a vague feeling.
- Structure paragraphs as miniature papers: topic sentence, developed body, sentence that closes the loop.
- Remember that English scientific prose puts the connective burden on the *writer*, not the reader — make every logical link explicit rather than assuming the reader will supply it.
