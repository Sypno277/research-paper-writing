# Chapter 4: Planning and Drafting

**Primary sources:** *The Craft of Research*, ch. 12; *A Manual for Writers* (Turabian), ch. 6–7; *Schaum's Quick Guide*, ch. 12–13

## Core Idea

Time spent planning before drafting always pays off — but the plan must be organized around **your readers' needs**, not around the chronology of your research process or the structure of your sources. The central planning tool both Booth et al. and Turabian teach is the **storyboard**: a sketch of your introduction, section-by-section reasons and evidence, and conclusion, built *before* you write continuous prose.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The Storyboard Method
Rather than starting with a linear outline, sketch a "storyboard" — one page per major section — containing:

**Introduction page:**
1. A brief summary of only the *most relevant* sources (the ones you'll challenge, modify, or extend) — never in the order you happened to read them; order by chronology, quality, significance, or point of view.
2. Your research question rephrased as a **gap or flaw** in that existing work ("Few of these historians, however, have explained why…").
3. A sketch of the answer to *So what if we don't find out?*
4. Your tentative main claim (to be placed either at the end of the introduction, or reserved for the conclusion — see below).

**Body pages:** one per reason, containing evidence, anticipated acknowledgments/responses, and warrants sketched in (see Chapter 3 for these elements).

**Conclusion page:** restate your claim, then sketch its broader significance (another pass at *So what?*).

This lets you see your whole argument's shape before committing to sentence-level prose — and reveals gaps (a reason with no evidence sketched in, a claim with no warrant) while they're still cheap to fix.

### The Claim-Placement Decision
A structural choice with real consequences: **where do you reveal your main claim?**
- **End of introduction (recommended default):** puts readers in control — they know your problem and its solution up front, can judge relevance as they read, and remember your point better. Most readers prefer this.
- **Reserved for the conclusion:** creates a "follow me to the reveal" relationship. Riskier — readers may lose the thread or feel controlled rather than informed. Requires, at minimum, a *launching sentence* at the end of the introduction naming the key terms that will recur throughout, so readers aren't reading blind.

New writers often fear stating their claim early will "bore" readers or feel repetitive. Booth et al. call both fears baseless: an interesting question makes readers want to see *how well* you answer it, not just *whether* you do.

### Three Flawed Plans to Avoid (Booth et al. / Turabian — near-identical framework)
1. **The narrative-of-your-thinking plan.** Organizing the paper around *what you did* (*"The first issue was… Then I compared… Finally I conclude…"*) rather than around your claim and reasons. Readers don't want your research diary; they want your argument. **Diagnostic:** scan your draft for process-narrating language like this — if it recurs, you're telling the story of finding your claim instead of arguing for it.
2. **The patchwork-of-sources plan.** Stringing together quotations, summaries, and paraphrases with little of your own analysis in between ("patch writing"). This is a plagiarism risk as well as a weak-argument risk, especially when research is done by cutting and pasting online. Even advanced researchers can fall into a subtler version: mapping their paper's organization onto a single major source's structure rather than building an organization that serves their own argument.
3. **The mirror-the-assignment plan.** Echoing the exact language and issue-order of an assignment prompt signals to a reader (especially a teacher) that you've contributed no independent thinking. If asked to "compare and contrast X and Y," don't default to a two-part X-then-Y structure (which tends to produce two disconnected summaries) — break the comparison into its conceptual sub-parts and organize around those instead.

### Converting a Storyboard into a Formal Outline (Turabian)
If your field or instructor requires a formal outline:
- **I.** = a complete sentence stating your claim.
- **II., III., …** = complete sentences, each stating one reason.
- **A., B., …** (under each reason) = sentences summarizing evidence; numbered items below list the specific evidence itself.

Using complete sentences rather than topic fragments at every level forces you to state an actual claim at each point, not just a label — a fragment like "II. Classroom technology" commits you to nothing; "II. Different uses of classroom technology have different effects on learning outcomes" does.

## Key Concepts & Definitions

- **Storyboard:** a page-per-section sketch of the argument's structure, drafted before continuous prose.
- **Launching sentence:** the sentence ending an introduction that reserves the claim for the conclusion but still names the key terms/concepts the paper will develop, so readers aren't left directionless.
- **Road map:** an explicit "In part 1, I discuss X; part 2 addresses Y…" sentence — common and expected in social sciences, often seen as clumsy in the humanities. Useful as a drafting aid even if cut from the final version.
- **Patch writing:** assembling a paper primarily from strung-together quotations/paraphrases with minimal original analysis between them.

## Mental Models & Metaphors

- **"Research is like gold mining: dig up a lot, pick out a little, toss the rest."** Discarding good material that isn't the *best* material is a sign of strong writing, not wasted effort (Booth et al., citing Hemingway).
- **The storyboard as a rehearsal space** — cheap to revise before you've committed to full sentences, the way a film storyboard is cheap to redraw before shooting begins.

## Common Pitfalls / Anti-patterns

- **Believing an outline alone is enough, then "grinding out sentences."** Experienced writers treat drafting itself as an act of discovery — plans reveal gaps, but writing reveals more. Expect your plan to change as you draft.
- **Summarizing marginally relevant sources in the introduction.** Only summarize the sources you intend to challenge, modify, or build on directly.
- **Warrant placement mistake:** if a warrant is needed, state it *before* the reason and claim it supports, not after — readers need the general principle in hand before they can accept the specific inference (see worked example).
- **Defaulting to assignment-prompt order** for a compare/contrast task, producing two disconnected halves instead of an integrated argument.

## Worked Example: Warrant Placement

Without a stated warrant (unclear to non-experts):
> Since most students at Oxford University in 1580 signed documents with only their first and last names, most of them must have been commoners.

With the warrant stated *first*:
> In late sixteenth-century England, a commoner did not add "Mr." or "Esq." to his signature. [warrant] Most students at Oxford in 1580 signed with only first and last names, [reason] so most of them must have been commoners. [claim]

## Key Takeaways

- Build a storyboard (intro page, one page per reason, conclusion page) before drafting continuous prose.
- Default to stating your main claim at the end of your introduction; reserve it for the conclusion only deliberately, and always supply a launching sentence if you do.
- Check your own draft for the three flawed-plan symptoms: process-narration language, patchwork quotation strings, and assignment-mirroring structure.
- If you use a formal outline, write every level as a complete sentence, not a topic fragment.
- Treat drafting itself as part of the discovery process — your plan will and should evolve as you write.
