# Chapter 17: Revising and Editing

**Primary sources:** *Writing Science* (Schimel), ch. 17; *A Manual for Writers* (Turabian), ch. 9; *English for Writing Research Papers* (Wallwork), ch. 20; *Schaum's Quick Guide*, ch. 20

## Core Idea

First drafts almost never have a single, isolated problem — they have **tangled, interacting problems**: a weak story structure travels together with poor transitions; jargon travels with nominalizations; unclear logic travels with clunky sentences. Untangling this requires multiple deliberate passes, each targeting a different level of the writing, moving from the largest structural issues down to the smallest word-choice details — never the reverse.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The SCFL Revision Sequence (Schimel)
> **S**tructure → **C**larity → **F**low → **L**anguage

1. **Structure:** get the story's shape right first — are the OCAR elements (Chapter 15) all present and correctly sequenced?
2. **Clarity:** are the ideas themselves clear and concrete, independent of polish?
3. **Flow:** do ideas link smoothly from one to the next (Chapter 16's Janus function, transitions)?
4. **Language:** does it sound good at the sentence and word level — the final layer of polish.

**Critical caveat: these passes are not strictly separable.** You can't fully fix structure while ignoring clarity, because an unclear topic makes structural problems hard to even diagnose; poor language often turns out to be the root cause of an apparent flow problem. Expect to cycle through SCFL multiple times — clarifying language may force you to reconsider structure, not just polish prose. This sequence is a **prioritization principle** (fix the biggest things first) more than a rigid four-pass checklist.

This directly parallels **Booth et al.'s top-down revision sequence from Chapter 5** (frame → argument substance/quality → organization → paragraphs → sentences/mechanics) — both frameworks independently converge on the same core insight: revise big-to-small, never small-to-big, because polishing sentences you'll later cut or restructure wastes real effort.

### Practical Discipline: Print It Out (Wallwork)
A concrete, low-tech but consistently effective technique: **print your paper and correct it on paper**, rather than only reading and editing on screen. Reading in a different physical medium changes how errors and awkward passages register — problems that are easy to skim past on screen often become visible on a printed page. Read the full document start to finish without stopping to fix minor issues on the first pass; note bigger structural or clarity problems as you go, save polishing for a subsequent pass.

### Always Revise with the Reader (or Referee) in Mind
Anticipate specifically how a reviewer or referee will react to weak spots — not just "is this correct" but "will this read as awkward, unclear, or amateurish to someone evaluating it critically?" This is especially important for non-native English writers: **anticipate that referees will comment on English quality** even when the science is sound, and weigh professional proofreading or editing services for high-stakes submissions if resources allow. If a professional proofreader's suggestions seem wrong, the right move is to go back and ask them to clarify or reconsider — not simply to override them without checking, since professional editors often catch genuine problems a writer is too close to see.

### A Practical Editing Checklist (synthesizing Wallwork, Turabian, Schaum)
When doing a final revision pass, work through the following systematically:
- **Cut, cut, and keep cutting.** Revisit Chapter 7's concision techniques — most first drafts can lose 10–20% of their words without losing content.
- **Check readability** — read passages aloud or have someone else read them; awkward phrasing often reveals itself more clearly this way than through silent reading.
- **Check the logical order** — does each section, paragraph, and sentence follow the one before it in a way a reader can track without backtracking?
- **Be careful with cut-and-paste edits** — moved text frequently leaves behind dangling transitions, duplicated points, or now-orphaned references ("as mentioned above" pointing to text that's since moved).
- **Double-check you've followed your target venue's formatting/submission requirements** exactly (see Chapter 20).
- **Verify factual and numerical accuracy** — a revision pass is also the last real chance to catch data-entry errors, misremembered citations, or miscalculated figures before submission.
- **Check consistency** — terminology, abbreviations, notation, and citation formatting should be uniform throughout, not drifting in usage across sections written at different times.
- **Confirm every source is credited** — cross-check that every quotation, paraphrase, or borrowed idea has a citation (see Chapter 8), and that your citation list has no orphaned or missing entries (see Chapter 9–10).
- **Don't forget the Acknowledgments** — funding sources, technical assistance, and non-authorial contributors are often owed formal recognition that's easy to forget in a final rush.

### Dealing with Rejection and Editorial Feedback
Take editorial and reviewer comments seriously rather than defensively — even critical or harshly phrased feedback usually contains a legitimate signal about what confused or unconvinced a reader. Don't be afraid to make **significant** revisions during editing, not just cosmetic ones; a willingness to substantially restructure in response to feedback, rather than only polishing surface wording, is what separates writers who improve across drafts from those who don't.

## Key Concepts & Definitions

- **SCFL:** Structure, Clarity, Flow, Language — Schimel's prioritized revision-pass sequence.
- **Top-down revision:** the shared principle (Schimel, Booth et al., Turabian) of fixing structural issues before sentence-level polish.
- **Real editing:** Schimel's term for the actual, iterative, multi-pass work of untangling interacting problems in a draft, as opposed to a single superficial proofread.

## Mental Models & Metaphors

- **Writing problems as environmental contamination** — Schimel's opening analogy: real-world contaminated sites rarely have a single pollutant; treating one problem can reveal or even create another. First drafts are the same — a "toxic" mix of structural, clarity, and language problems that requires multiple targeted passes, not one cure-all fix.
- **Printing on paper as a perspective shift** — physically changing the reading medium to interrupt the automatic pattern-completion that lets errors slide by unnoticed on screen.

## Common Pitfalls / Anti-patterns

- **Doing only one revision pass** and expecting it to catch structural, clarity, flow, and language problems simultaneously.
- **Polishing sentence-level language before structural issues are resolved** — wasted effort if that material gets cut or reorganized later.
- **Only reading on-screen**, missing errors that a printed read-through would surface.
- **Leaving cut-and-paste artifacts** (dangling transitions, orphaned cross-references) after reorganizing a draft.
- **Dismissing professional or peer editing feedback without seriously engaging with it**, especially around English-language quality for non-native writers.
- **Treating "revision" as only fixing typos**, rather than as an opportunity for substantial restructuring when the feedback calls for it.

## Key Takeaways

- Revise big-to-small: structure first, then clarity, then flow, then language — expect to cycle through this more than once.
- Print out your draft and read it in a different medium at least once before final submission.
- Anticipate a critical reader's reaction, not just your own sense that "this makes sense to me."
- Work through a systematic final checklist: cutting, readability, logical order, cut-paste artifacts, formatting compliance, factual accuracy, consistency, and complete source crediting.
- Take rejection and editorial feedback as genuine signal, and be willing to make substantial — not just cosmetic — changes in response.
