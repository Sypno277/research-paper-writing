# Chapter 20: Paper Format, Submission, and Presentations

**Primary sources:** *A Manual for Writers* (Turabian), Appendix, ch. 13; *Schaum's Quick Guide*, ch. 19, 21

## Core Idea

A paper's content can be excellent and still lose credibility if its physical formatting is inconsistent or ignores institutional requirements — and a research project's life doesn't end at the written page. Oral presentations, posters, and conference proposals require translating the same underlying argument into fundamentally different formats, because **listeners are not readers**: they can't pause, reread, or scan for subheadings, so a talk must be structured differently from the paper it's based on.

## Frameworks Introduced

### General Format Requirements (Class Papers, Theses, Dissertations)
These are Turabian's defaults for US academic formatting — **always check your specific institution's or journal's local guidelines first**, since these frequently override general defaults:
- **Margins:** at least one inch on all four edges of standard 8½×11" pages; a thesis/dissertation intended for binding may need a larger left margin (often 1½ inches).
- **Typeface:** a single, readable, widely available font (Times New Roman, Courier, Helvetica); avoid ornamental fonts, which can undermine a paper's perceived seriousness. At least 10-point, preferably 12-point, for body text.
- **Spacing:** double-space body text; **single-space** block quotations, table titles/figure captions, and appendix lists. Use single spacing *within* certain front-matter elements (table of contents, footnotes/endnotes, bibliography) but with a blank line *between* entries. One space (not two) after terminal sentence punctuation. Use tabs/indents for paragraph indentation, not manual spaces.
- **Pagination:** number body pages and back matter with arabic numerals; front matter (before the body) typically uses lowercase roman numerals; an unnumbered title page (if it's the only front matter) is still counted in the sequence even though the number doesn't print on it.

### Front Matter Elements (Thesis/Dissertation Context)
Depending on institutional requirements, formal academic documents may include, roughly in this order: a submission page, title page, copyright page, dedication, epigraph, table of contents, abstract, preface, acknowledgments, and (if needed) a glossary or a note on editorial/research method. **Not every paper needs all of these** — class papers typically need far fewer — but knowing the full menu helps you check what your specific context requires rather than guessing.

### Planning an Oral Presentation
The core insight governing everything else in this section: **listeners process information fundamentally differently from readers.** Readers can pause to reflect, scan subheadings to track structure, and reread when their attention wanders. Listeners cannot — they get one linear pass through your material in real time, with no ability to "look back" the way a reader can flip to an earlier page.

**Practical consequence:** a talk cannot simply be a paper read aloud. It must be restructured for real-time, linear, unrepeatable delivery — clearer explicit signposting, fewer embedded sub-arguments, more redundancy at key transition points than would be tolerable in written prose.

**Three ways to narrow your focus for a time-limited talk** (a typical class or conference slot is ~20 minutes, which — if reading from a script, a generally discouraged practice — caps you around 7–10 double-spaced pages):
1. **Problem statement with a sketch of the argument** — best when your problem itself is novel; open with a brief literature review, the question, the consequences of not knowing the answer, and your claim (echoing the Context-Problem-Response structure from Chapter 13), then briefly summarize your reasons and evidence for each.
2. **Summary of one sub-argument** — best when your full argument is too large for the time slot; mention the larger problem in your introduction and conclusion, but explicitly signal you're addressing only one part of it.
3. **Methodology or data report** — best when your primary contribution is a new method or data source; explain briefly why it matters, then focus the talk on how the new method/data solves the stated problem.

### Additional Presentation Components (from Turabian's fuller chapter)
Beyond structuring the talk itself, a complete presentation plan includes: preparing for likely audience questions in advance (anticipate the same kinds of objections covered in Chapter 3's acknowledgment/response framework); and creating handouts that let the audience retain key details (data, citations, complex figures) they can't fully absorb from a single spoken pass. **Posters** are a hybrid form combining written and spoken elements — designed to be read somewhat independently (like a compressed paper) while also supporting a live, conversational walkthrough with interested viewers. A **conference proposal** (the abstract or extended abstract submitted to be considered for a talk slot — see Chapter 12) is itself a persuasive document, and should be written with the same rhetorical care as the paper itself, since it's your only chance to earn the invitation to present in the first place.

## Key Concepts & Definitions

- **Front matter:** the formal preliminary elements (title page, table of contents, abstract, etc.) preceding the body of a thesis or dissertation.
- **Listener vs. reader processing:** the core distinction driving all oral-presentation design choices — listeners can't pause, reread, or scan ahead.
- **Poster:** a hybrid written/spoken presentation format, readable independently but also designed for live conversational explanation.

## Mental Models & Metaphors

- **A talk as a one-pass, real-time medium** — unlike a paper, which a reader can navigate non-linearly (skip ahead, flip back, reread a hard passage), a talk only ever happens once, forward, at a fixed pace set by the speaker, not the audience.

## Common Pitfalls / Anti-patterns

- **Reformatting a written paper's argument for oral delivery without restructuring it** — reading a dense written paragraph aloud, verbatim, is one of the most common ways presenters lose an audience.
- **Ignoring institution- or journal-specific formatting requirements** in favor of generic defaults, when the two conflict.
- **Overloading a time-limited talk** by trying to cover the paper's full argument rather than choosing one of the three narrowing strategies.
- **Presenting a wall of data slides with no more narrative structure than "and now this slide shows..."** — treating passive listeners as if they were active, rereading readers.
- **Underinvesting in the conference proposal/abstract itself**, treating it as a formality rather than the actual persuasive document that determines whether you're invited to present at all.
- **Skipping question preparation** — arriving unprepared for the kinds of objections a live audience is likely to raise.

## Key Takeaways

- Always verify institution- or venue-specific formatting requirements before applying general defaults for margins, typeface, spacing, and front matter.
- Remember that listeners and readers process information differently — restructure, don't just recite, when moving from paper to talk.
- Choose one of three narrowing strategies (problem+argument sketch, one sub-argument, or methodology/data focus) to fit your argument into a limited time slot.
- Prepare for audience questions in advance, using the same acknowledgment/response thinking developed for written arguments.
- Treat conference proposals and abstracts as genuinely persuasive documents, not administrative formalities.
