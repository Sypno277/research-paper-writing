# Chapter 9: Citations — Chicago Notes-Bibliography and Author-Date Style

**Primary source:** *A Manual for Writers of Research Papers, Theses, and Dissertations* (Turabian), ch. 15–19

## Core Idea

Chicago style offers **two parallel, mutually exclusive systems** for citing sources — Notes-Bibliography (common in humanities) and Author-Date (common in sciences and social sciences). Both answer the same four underlying questions about every source (who created it, what identifies it, who published it and when, where can it be found) — they just present that information in different places and formats. Pick one system per paper based on your field's convention, and apply it with total consistency.

## Frameworks Introduced

### Four Reasons to Cite (Turabian)
1. **Give credit** — recognition is often a researcher's primary reward.
2. **Assure readers of factual accuracy** — readers trust your facts only if they can trace and verify your sources.
3. **Show your research tradition** — citations place your work in conversation with the field, not just support isolated claims.
4. **Help readers extend your work** — many readers use your citation list as a map for their own further research.

### Four Questions Every Citation Must Answer
Regardless of style or source type, a complete citation identifies:
1. **Who** wrote, edited, or translated the source?
2. **What** identifies it — title, subtitle, journal/series name, volume/edition, specific page range?
3. **Who published it, and when** — publisher, place, date (or "unpublished" if applicable)?
4. **Where can it be found** — for print, this is implicit; for online sources, a URL or database name; for archival material, the housing collection.

### System 1: Notes-Bibliography Style
Used widely in humanities. Two components:
- **In-text:** a superscript number at the end of the sentence referencing the source.
- **Note (footnote or endnote):** a correspondingly numbered note with full citation details.
- **Bibliography (end of paper):** an alphabetized list, one entry per source, in a slightly different format from the note.

**Generic template — book (single author), full note:**
> `N: #. First Last, *Title: Subtitle* (Place: Publisher, Year), page.`
> Example pattern: `1. Jane Author, *The Long Title: A Subtitle* (Chicago: Sample Press, 2020), 45.`

**Generic template — same book, bibliography entry (name inverted, periods not commas):**
> `B: Last, First. *Title: Subtitle*. Place: Publisher, Year.`

**Shortened note (after the first full citation of a source):**
> `N: #. Last, *Short Title*, page.`

Once you've cited a source in full, all later references to it use this shortened form — repeating the full citation every time clutters the page.

### System 2: Author-Date Style
Used widely in sciences and social sciences. Two components:
- **In-text:** a parenthetical citation with author's last name, year, and (if quoting a specific passage) page number — no separate note.
- **Reference list (end of paper):** one alphabetized entry per source, containing the full bibliographic data the parenthetical citation only gestures toward.

**Generic template — book, parenthetical in-text citation:**
> `(Author Year, page)` — e.g., `(Author 2020, 45)`. No punctuation between author and year; a comma separates year from page.

**Generic template — same book, reference-list entry:**
> `Last, First. Year. *Title: Subtitle*. Place: Publisher.`

**Key structural difference from Notes-Bibliography:** the reference-list entry's elements are always in the order **author → date → title → other publication facts**, and most elements are separated by periods.

### Choosing and Applying a System
- **Order of elements** in a reference-list/bibliography entry is fixed by style, not by convenience — don't improvise the order.
- **Capitalization:** most English-language titles use headline style (major words capitalized); foreign-language titles typically use sentence style (only the first word and proper nouns capitalized) — check your field's local convention, since some require sentence style even for English titles except journal/magazine/newspaper names.
- **Consistency across the whole paper** matters more than any single formatting micro-decision — a reviewer will forgive a rare inconsistency more readily than a document that mixes systems.

## Key Concepts & Definitions

- **Full note vs. shortened note:** the first citation of a source in Notes-Bibliography style is complete; subsequent citations use an abbreviated form (last name, short title, page).
- **Ibid.:** a Latin abbreviation ("in the same place") historically used for a note citing the same source as the immediately preceding note — increasingly discouraged in favor of simple shortened forms, since digital documents make "immediately preceding" ambiguous when notes are reordered or edited.
- **Reference list vs. bibliography:** functionally similar (both list full source data at the paper's end), but a *reference list* (Author-Date) should generally include only sources actually cited, while a *bibliography* (Notes-Bibliography) may also include works consulted but not directly cited.
- **Parenthetical citation:** an in-text citation in parentheses, used in Author-Date style, containing minimally author and year.

## Mental Models & Metaphors

- **Citations as an address, not a summary** — their job is purely to let a reader locate the exact source, not to describe or evaluate it. Precision and consistency matter more than elegance here.
- **Templates as reusable scaffolding** — building a personal template file for the 4–5 source types you cite most often (book, journal article, chapter in edited volume, website, government document) saves far more time over a project's life than looking each one up individually every time.

## Common Pitfalls / Anti-patterns

- **Mixing Notes-Bibliography and Author-Date within one paper.** Pick one, based on field convention, and use it throughout.
- **Improvising element order** in a bibliography/reference-list entry instead of following the fixed template.
- **Forgetting that a full citation is needed the *first* time a source appears**, even if you plan to use a shortened form later.
- **Treating "Ibid." as always safe** — ambiguous once notes get reordered or new citations inserted between drafts; many style guides now discourage it in favor of explicit shortened citations.
- **Citing only what you quoted, forgetting sources you paraphrased or summarized** — paraphrase and summary require citation exactly as quotation does (see Chapter 8).

## Key Takeaways

- Determine early which system (Notes-Bibliography or Author-Date) your field and target venue expects, and apply it consistently from your first draft.
- Every citation, in either system, must answer who/what/who-published-when/where-found.
- Build personal templates for your most-used source types rather than re-deriving citation formats from scratch each time.
- Use full citations on first use and shortened forms thereafter — but keep the shortened form unambiguous.
- A reference list should generally mirror your in-text citations exactly (one entry per cited source, no orphans in either direction).
