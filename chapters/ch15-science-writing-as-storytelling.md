# Chapter 15: Science Writing as Storytelling — The OCAR Framework

**Primary source:** *Writing Science: How to Write Papers That Get Cited and Proposals That Get Funded* (Schimel), ch. 2–9

## Core Idea

A scientific paper is not a neutral presentation of data — **it is a story**, built from data but not identical to it. The papers that get cited most and the proposals that get funded most are the ones that tell the most compelling stories. Every story, scientific or otherwise, is built from four functional elements — **Opening, Challenge, Action, Resolution (OCAR)** — and understanding how to manage those elements is, in Schimel's framing, "at the heart of successful writing," operating at every scale from a whole paper down to a single paragraph or sentence.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The Four Elements of Every Story: OCAR
- **Opening (O):** Who/what is the story about? Where does it take place? What context does the reader need to follow the story? What is the larger problem being addressed?
- **Challenge (C):** What do the characters need to accomplish? What specific question does the work propose to answer?
- **Action (A):** What happens to address the challenge? In a paper, this is the work you did; in a proposal, the work you hope to do.
- **Resolution (R):** How have the characters and their world changed as a result of the action? This is your conclusion — what did you learn?

A story missing any of these four elements will feel unsatisfying and "slippery" rather than memorable — sticky. This isn't a metaphorical add-on to scientific writing; it's the literal underlying shape of how a paper's Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion function together.

### Why Scientists' Stories Often Don't Land
Schimel opens by addressing a real critique (from journalist Elizabeth Kolbert) that scientists "don't tell stories." His diagnosis: scientists *do* tell stories, but three factors obscure this — (1) science uses a formalized structure unfamiliar to non-scientist readers, so the story gets lost in translation; (2) many scientists are simply weak storytellers, either not seeing their own story clearly or failing to convey it; (3) many scientists actively resist framing their work as "storytelling," worried it implies a loss of objectivity. Schimel's counter: telling a good science story is *not* about imposing a predetermined narrative onto data — that's a real failure mode (jamming data into a story decided in advance) — but about *listening to your data as characters* and letting the story emerge from careful, open-minded analysis, exactly the way a fiction writer is advised to listen to characters rather than force them through a predetermined plot.

### Four Core Story Structures, Ranked by Audience Patience
Different genres of story (and different scientific writing contexts) front-load the OCAR elements to different degrees, depending on how patient the intended audience is:

1. **OCAR (in sequence)** — the slowest-developing structure; elements appear roughly in order (Opening, then Challenge, then Action, then Resolution). This is the structure **most scientific papers use**, because scientific readers are patient: they want to assess ideas and evidence as they develop, not be handed a conclusion up front. The challenge appears at the end of the Introduction; the conclusion comes at the very end.

2. **ABDCE** — used by proposal writers and modern fiction: **A**ction (a dramatic opening event to hook the reader) → **B**ackground (fills in characters/setting, effectively O and C combined retroactively) → **D**evelopment → **C**limax → **E**nding (= resolution). This front-loads engagement by opening with action before providing context — good for less patient audiences, like grant reviewers skimming many proposals, at the cost of some narrative efficiency (you have to circle back and fill in background after the exciting opening).

3. **LD (Lead/Development)** — the newspaper "inverted pyramid": the entire O-C-R gets compressed into the opening sentences (the *lead*), with everything after simply filling out detail. Developed under the physical constraint that editors might cut a story's final paragraphs to fit space — so the point can never be saved for the end.

4. **LDR (Lead/Development/Resolution)** — magazine journalism's variant: similar front-loading via a strong lead, but with more room and expectation for a genuine, crafted resolution at the end, since magazine readers who start an article are likelier to finish it than newspaper skimmers.

**These four sit on a continuum of audience patience** — from OCAR (most patient — build the whole case before concluding) to LD (least patient — lead with the conclusion immediately). Choosing the right structure for a piece of scientific writing means asking: how patient is *this* audience? A specialist-journal paper, a general-audience science article, and a grant proposal each warrant a different point on this continuum.

### The Resolution Must Close the Loop — and the Loop Is a Spiral, Not a Circle
Whichever structure you use, **a story without a resolution fails** — readers (or reviewers) need to know how the characters and their understanding have changed. Because the ending brings genuine change, a satisfying story is not a literal circle returning to its starting point — it's a **spiral**: it returns to the same *topic* raised in the Opening, but from a changed position. A science paper's resolution must map back explicitly to the problem identified in the Opening — restating (with new understanding) why the original problem mattered, now that the work has been done. A paper that never does this — that just stops after presenting results — reads as unresolved, unsatisfying, and (in practical terms) is a strong candidate for rejection.

### Mapping OCAR onto IMRaD (Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion)
Most scientific papers are physically structured as IMRaD, not literally labeled "Opening/Challenge/Action/Resolution" — but the **conceptual** structure underneath is OCAR regardless of the paper's surface section labels. Schimel explicitly frames this distinction: **IMRaD is a rule** (a convention for how sections are physically labeled and ordered, which varies across fields and sometimes doesn't apply at all — review papers and proposals often skip IMRaD entirely) — **OCAR is a principle** (the underlying narrative logic every piece of scientific writing must satisfy, regardless of its physical section structure).

The mapping:
- **Introduction** contains three OCAR sub-parts, rarely labeled as such: an **Opening** paragraph introducing the larger problem and its context/characters; **Background** that extends the Opening by giving readers what they need to understand the specific work and its stakes; and a **Challenge** stating the specific hypotheses/questions/goals.
- **Methods** begins the **Action** — what you did.
- **Results** continues the **Action** — what you found.
- **Discussion** builds toward the **Resolution** — what it all means — typically culminating in a Conclusions subsection that *is* the Resolution.

### The Hourglass Shape of a Paper
A direct consequence of the OCAR/IMRaD mapping: a well-built scientific paper has an **hourglass shape**. It opens wide (a problem of broad interest), narrows sharply through the Challenge into the Methods and Results (the narrowest point — specific, technical, precise), then widens back out through the Discussion into Conclusions that reconnect to a topic as broad as the one raised in the Opening. **The conclusions should address a scope as wide as the opening did** — this is precisely what makes the story circular (or rather, spiral): the resolution returns explicitly to the issue the paper opened with, now transformed by what was learned.

## Key Concepts & Definitions

- **OCAR:** Opening, Challenge, Action, Resolution — the four universal story elements, operating at every scale of scientific writing.
- **Character (in science writing):** whatever a passage's narrative centers on — not necessarily a person; can be a molecule, an ecosystem, a gene, a concept.
- **Audience patience continuum:** the spectrum from OCAR (most patient, slowest reveal) through ABDCE and LD to LDR, determining how much front-loading a piece of writing needs.
- **Hourglass structure:** the shape of a well-built paper — wide opening, narrow methods/results, wide resolution.
- **Spiral (not circle) resolution:** a story returns to its opening topic, but transformed by what happened in between.

## Mental Models & Metaphors

- **Data as "characters" to be listened to, not pawns to be arranged.** Schimel explicitly borrows this framing from fiction-writing advice (Anne Lamott): impose a predetermined plot on your characters and the story feels false; let the story emerge from careful attention to what the characters (data) actually do, and it stays honest *and* compelling.
- **The hourglass** as a visual model for how scope should narrow and then re-expand across a paper's structure.
- **The spiral, not the circle** — a resolution that returns to the same *place* it started would mean nothing changed; a resolution that returns to the same *topic*, transformed, is what makes an ending satisfying.

## Common Pitfalls / Anti-patterns

- **Deciding on your "story" before analyzing the data, then forcing the data to fit it** — the single failure mode Schimel identifies as undermining both the science and the storytelling.
- **Treating "storytelling" as inherently unscientific or unobjective** — a widespread but mistaken belief among researchers that this chapter directly rebuts; a story that grows organically from evidence and existing theory is fully compatible with rigor.
- **Writing a paper (or proposal) with no genuine resolution** — presenting results and stopping, without reconnecting to the original problem's significance.
- **Mismatching structure to audience patience** — using a slow-building OCAR structure for an impatient grant-reviewer audience, or an action-first ABDCE hook for a specialist audience that wants to see the evidence build methodically before any claims are made.
- **A resolution narrower in scope than the opening** — failing to widen back out at the end, leaving the paper feeling like it never answered why the original problem mattered.

## Key Takeaways

- Every piece of scientific writing — paper, proposal, section, even individual paragraph — should satisfy all four OCAR elements: Opening, Challenge, Action, Resolution.
- Match your story structure (OCAR, ABDCE, LD, or LDR) to your actual audience's patience — specialist journal readers tolerate a slow build; grant reviewers and general audiences often need faster front-loading.
- Let your story emerge from your data through careful, open-minded analysis — never decide the narrative first and then select data to fit it.
- Remember that IMRaD is just the physical container; OCAR is the narrative logic that must be satisfied inside it, regardless of which sections are labeled what.
- Build your paper's scope like an hourglass: wide problem → narrow technical core → wide resolution that reconnects explicitly to the opening problem.
