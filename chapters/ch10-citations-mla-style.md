# Chapter 10: Citations — MLA Style

**Primary source:** *Schaum's Quick Guide to Writing Great Research Papers* (Rozakis), ch. 16–18

**A note on currency:** MLA style is actively maintained by the Modern Language Association and has been revised across several editions (the current is the MLA Handbook, 9th edition). The specific formatting conventions below reflect the general *pattern* MLA has used for author-page in-text citation and a Works Cited list — a pattern that has stayed stable even as specific rules (especially for digital/online sources) have been updated. **Always verify current details against the latest MLA Handbook or your institution's style guide** before submitting a paper — this chapter should be treated as a map of the underlying logic, not a substitute for the current official rules.

## Core Idea

MLA style — standard in literature, languages, and many humanities disciplines — uses **brief parenthetical in-text citations** (author and page number, not date) paired with a full **Works Cited** list at the paper's end. It is structurally similar to Chicago's Author-Date style but swaps "date" for "page number" as the second in-text element, reflecting the humanities' emphasis on locating a specific passage in a text rather than dating a claim.

## Frameworks Introduced

### MLA In-Text (Parenthetical) Citation Patterns
The first time you cite a source, work in enough identifying information — author's name, and often the work's title if you're discussing multiple works by the same author — for the reader to find the matching Works Cited entry. After that, keep citations minimal.

**Naming the author in your own sentence, citing only the page:**
> According to [Author], [claim/paraphrase] ("quoted material if any") (42).

**Not naming the author in your sentence — full parenthetical:**
> [claim, paraphrased or quoted] (LastName 42).

**Source with no identifiable author:**
> Cite by a shortened form of the title instead of an author name, e.g., ("Short Title" 12) — or, for a web source with no page numbers, cite by author/title alone and let the Works Cited entry carry the URL.

**Citing an indirect source** (a quotation you found quoted inside another source, not the original):
> Use "qtd. in" to signal you're citing someone's citation, not the primary text itself: (Original Author qtd. in Secondary Author 21). Whenever possible, locate and cite the primary source directly instead — indirect citation should be a last resort when the original genuinely can't be found.

### The Works Cited List
Every source cited in-text must have a corresponding entry; every Works Cited entry must be cited somewhere in the text (no orphans in either direction — this matches the Chapter 9 principle for Chicago's reference list).

**General book entry pattern:**
> Last, First. *Title: Subtitle*. Publisher, Year.

**General periodical article entry pattern:**
> Last, First. "Article Title." *Periodical Title*, vol. #, no. #, Date, pp. XX–YY.

**Online/web source — core elements to capture** (specific formatting evolves, but the underlying information you must gather doesn't):
- Author (distinguish the actual content author from a web designer or site maintainer; if no author is identifiable, begin the entry with the title instead).
- Title of the specific document, and the title of the larger site/publication it's part of, if different.
- Date of publication or most recent revision (use the most recent date if both exist; use "n.d." — no date — if neither is available).
- URL or DOI (a DOI, when available, is more stable and preferred over a URL that may change).
- Date you accessed the source (increasingly optional in current MLA guidance for stable, dated sources, but still good practice for sources likely to change, like wikis or social media).

**Database article entry:** cite as you would the equivalent print article, then append the database name and any stable identifier (DOI or permalink) the database provides.

## Key Concepts & Definitions

- **In-text (parenthetical) citation:** the brief author-page marker placed directly in your sentence, MLA's substitute for footnotes/endnotes.
- **Works Cited:** the end-of-paper alphabetized list containing full bibliographic data for every in-text citation — narrower than a "Works Consulted" or "Bibliography" list, which may also include sources you read but didn't directly cite.
- **"qtd. in":** the signal phrase for citing a quotation at second hand, i.e., quoting someone's quotation of someone else, rather than the original source directly.

## Mental Models & Metaphors

- **MLA citations as a page-turn away, not a name-and-date away** — the emphasis on page numbers over publication dates reflects the humanities' core task: pointing a reader to an exact passage in a text for close reading, rather than dating an empirical claim.

## Common Pitfalls / Anti-patterns

- **Citing a web designer or site maintainer as "author"** instead of the actual content creator — Schaum's flags this as a common, avoidable error.
- **Over-relying on indirect ("qtd. in") citation** when the primary source is actually findable — always prefer citing the original directly.
- **Mismatched in-text citations and Works Cited entries** — every source cited in the text needs a matching entry, and vice versa.
- **Treating older printed MLA guidance (like the angle-bracket URL and mandatory access-date conventions once standard) as still current** without checking the latest edition — MLA's online-source rules in particular have changed significantly across editions.

## Key Takeaways

- MLA in-text citations use author + page number, not author + date — the opposite emphasis from Chicago Author-Date style.
- Every Works Cited entry must correspond to an actual in-text citation, and vice versa.
- For online sources, focus on capturing the underlying information (real author, title, date, stable locator) even as exact formatting conventions evolve between MLA editions.
- Use "qtd. in" only when you genuinely cannot access the original source being quoted.
- Always check the current MLA Handbook edition before finalizing formatting details — this chapter gives you the pattern, not a guarantee of current exact punctuation and ordering.
