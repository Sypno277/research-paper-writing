# Chapter 2: Finding, Evaluating, and Engaging Sources

**Primary sources:** *The Craft of Research*, ch. 5–6; *A Manual for Writers* (Turabian), ch. 3–4; *Schaum's Quick Guide*, ch. 5–11

## Core Idea

Source-gathering is not a passive collection exercise — it is an active search guided by your question, filtered by reliability criteria, and read *for a problem*, not just for information. The two biggest skills this chapter covers are (1) **classifying and evaluating** sources before you trust them, and (2) **reading actively** so that sources generate research problems for you rather than just facts to summarize.

## Frameworks Introduced

### Three Kinds of Sources (Booth et al.)
- **Primary sources** — "raw data": historical documents, literary texts, artworks, survey data, original research reports. What counts varies by field.
- **Secondary sources** — peer-reviewed books and articles built on primary sources, aimed at scholars. This is a field's "literature" — the conversation you're trying to join.
- **Tertiary sources** — textbooks, encyclopedia entries (including Wikipedia), general-audience articles that synthesize secondary sources. Useful for orientation early on; citing them in a scholarly argument marks you as a novice, because they oversimplify and date quickly.

The boundaries are fuzzy and field-dependent (a natural-science "primary source" report of original research functions differently than a historian's primary document), but the categories are useful for planning a search strategy: use tertiary sources to orient yourself, secondary sources to build your argument, and primary sources as the evidence base itself.

### Seven-Point Reliability Check (Booth et al.)
Before trusting any source, check:
1. **Reputable press?** University presses are generally reliable; be skeptical of commercial presses making sensational claims, especially on contested issues (climate, gun policy, etc.).
2. **Peer-reviewed?** If not, be cautious.
3. **Reputable author?** Check credentials; watch for undisclosed funding conflicts on contested topics.
4. **Reputable sponsor (if online-only)?** A website is only as reliable as its sponsor; check whether it's actively maintained.
5. **Current?** "Current" is field-relative — months in computer science, up to a decade in social sciences, decades in humanities. Check the works-cited dates in recent field publications to calibrate.
6. **Has notes/bibliography (if a book)?** Absence is a red flag — you can't verify anything it claims.
7. **Has bibliographic metadata (if a website)?** No sponsor/author/date information = unverifiable.

### Skim-to-Evaluate-Relevance Technique (Booth et al.)
Before reading a source in full, skim strategically to judge relevance:
- **Book:** index for your keywords → those pages; first/last paragraphs of keyword-heavy chapters; prologue/intro/summary chapters; last chapter's opening and closing pages; bibliography for related titles.
- **Article:** abstract; intro and conclusion (or first 6–7 and last 4–5 paragraphs if unmarked); section headings and their first/last paragraphs; bibliography.
- **Website:** same as article, plus "About," "Site Map," or built-in search for keywords.

This isn't just a time-saver — Booth et al. note it doubles as a lesson in your own writing: if your own paper can't survive being skimmed this way by a reader in a hurry, it has a structural problem (see Chapter 5, Organizing the Paper).

### Reading for a Problem: Creative Agreement & Creative Disagreement (Booth et al.)
This is the most distinctive and useful framework in this chapter. Once you have a candidate question, read sources *not just for information but to generate a research problem* — a gap, contradiction, or extension you can argue for.

**Creative agreement** — extend a source you find persuasive:
- *Offer additional/stronger support:* "Source X supports claim C with old/weak evidence, but I offer new/stronger evidence."
- *Confirm an unsupported claim:* "Source X assumes/speculates C is true; I can prove it."
- *Apply the claim more widely:* "Source X shows C is true in situation A; I show it's true generally (or in situation B)."

**Creative disagreement** — five reliable patterns for generating problems from sources you doubt:
1. **Contradiction of kind:** "Source claims X is a type of Y, but it's not" (e.g., "graffiti is vandalism" → "graffiti is public art").
2. **Part-whole contradiction:** "Source claims A is part of B in a certain way, but it isn't."
3. **Developmental/historical contradiction:** "Source claims X originated/changes in a certain way, but it doesn't."
4. **Cause-effect contradiction:** "Source claims X causes Y, but it doesn't / they share a common cause / X causes more than just Y."
5. **Contradiction of perspective:** "Source analyzes X only from viewpoint V, but a different viewpoint (social, ethical, historical, economic…) reveals something new." This is the most powerful pattern because it doesn't just correct a claim — it reframes the whole conversation.

## Key Concepts & Definitions

- **Systematic vs. aimless search:** aimless browsing can spark ideas but won't finish a project on deadline; you need a keyword/database plan once you have a working question.
- **"Hypothesis on probation"** (Peirce, via Booth): a working answer held loosely enough to be tested and abandoned.
- **Reading for arguments:** beyond reading for a problem, also read sources to find (a) objections you must acknowledge and answer, and (b) models of reasoning/analysis you can adapt to your own material.
- **Note discipline:** whatever your note-taking medium (index cards, word processor, dedicated app, reference manager), the non-negotiable rule is to **unambiguously distinguish** (1) direct quotation, (2) your paraphrase/summary, and (3) your own commentary — using different formatting, brackets, or colors. Confusing these three is the single most common mechanism of accidental plagiarism (see Chapter 8).

## Mental Models & Metaphors

- **Sources as a conversation you're joining**, not a warehouse of facts to extract. This reframes "finding sources" as "finding out who else is already arguing about this, and where the openings are."
- **The skim test as a mirror**: the way you'd skim someone else's source for relevance is the way a reader will skim *your* paper — so build your own paper to survive that treatment.

## Common Pitfalls / Anti-patterns

- **Trusting a tertiary source in an academic argument.** Fine for orientation, embarrassing as a cited authority.
- **Citing an old edition's outdated position** — researchers revise views between editions; always check you have the most recent edition of a secondary/tertiary source, though *primary* works (novels, letters) should use a standard, not necessarily newest, edition.
- **Reading only to confirm your own view.** Booth et al. stress reading a source *generously* first — to understand it on its own terms — before reading it critically a second time. Reading only for weaknesses risks misrepresenting the source (a documented, embarrassing failure mode even among established scholars).
- **Jumbling notes.** Mixing quotation, paraphrase, and your own thought in one undifferentiated blob makes both plagiarism and simple errors far more likely later.
- **Assuming disagreement weakens your paper.** New researchers often avoid mentioning opposing views, fearing it undercuts their argument. The opposite is true — see Chapter 3.

## Worked Example: Turning a Source into a Problem

> Smith (2019) argues that legalizing a practice will increase its use among a vulnerable population, citing correlational survey data.

Creative disagreement (cause-effect pattern): *Smith claims legalization causes increased use, but longitudinal data from comparable jurisdictions shows usage rates were already rising before legalization — the correlation may share a common cause (changing social attitudes) rather than reflecting a causal link.* This single move — noticing that Smith's evidence supports correlation but claims causation — is a fully formed research problem, ready to become the seed of a paper's argument.

## Key Takeaways

- Classify prospective sources as primary/secondary/tertiary before you decide how much weight to give them.
- Run the seven-point reliability check before citing anything, especially on contested topics.
- Skim strategically for relevance before committing to a full read.
- Read every promising source twice: once generously to understand it, once critically to find agreement to extend or disagreement to pursue.
- Keep quotation, paraphrase, and your own commentary visually and structurally separate in your notes — always.
