# Chapter 8: Incorporating Sources — Quoting, Paraphrasing, and Avoiding Plagiarism

**Primary sources:** *The Craft of Research*, ch. 14; *English for Writing Research Papers* (Wallwork), ch. 10; *A Manual for Writers* (Turabian), ch. 7, 25; *Schaum's Quick Guide*, ch. 14–15

## Core Idea

Plagiarism is rarely a matter of deliberate theft — it is overwhelmingly a matter of **losing track of which words are yours and which are borrowed**, especially during note-taking and paraphrasing. The single most reliable defense is procedural: keep quotation, paraphrase, and your own commentary visually distinct from the moment you take notes (Chapter 2), and apply concrete, mechanical tests — not intuition — when deciding whether a paraphrase is too close to its source.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The "Would They Recognize It?" Test (Booth et al.)
The clearest single diagnostic for whether something needs a citation: **if the person you borrowed from read your paper, would they recognize your words or ideas as theirs** — including paraphrases, summaries, or even a distinctive method or framing you adopted from them? If yes, cite. This test resolves most edge cases better than any rule of thumb about word counts.

### Citing Ideas vs. Common Knowledge
You don't need to cite an idea that is (1) not associated with a specific person, and (2) established enough to be part of a field's shared, common knowledge. "The brain has functionally distinct regions" needs no citation. "Emotions play a crucial causal role in rational decision-making" — a claim tied to specific, relatively recent researchers — does. The test is not *how well-known is this fact* in a vague sense, but whether the idea is (a) tied to an identifiable source and (b) not yet common knowledge in the field you're writing for.

### Signaling Quotations: The "Striking Phrase" Test
Even a single line copied verbatim needs quotation marks — but the harder judgment call is *ordinary* phrasing that happens to come from a source. Rule of thumb: put quotation marks around phrases that show **originality of thought or expression** ("technology begets more technology," "autocatalytic process"); don't bother quoting generic phrasing that carries no distinctive style ("the importance of an invention"). When genuinely uncertain, **err toward quoting** — Booth et al.'s framing: "it's better to seem naive than dishonest."

Once a distinctive phrase has been properly quoted and cited once, you may reuse the underlying *idea* afterward in your own words without repeating the quotation marks or citation each time — as long as it's clear you're still discussing the same source's idea.

### The "Look Away" Paraphrase Test (Booth et al.)
A practical procedure for producing a genuinely non-plagiarizing paraphrase:
1. Read the source passage.
2. **Look away from it entirely.**
3. Still not looking at it, write your paraphrase from memory, in your own words.
4. Check: can you run your finger along your sentence and find a synonym-for-synonym match with the source, in the same order? If yes, the paraphrase is too close — try again.

**Worked example (from Gladwell's *Outliers*, original):**
> "Achievement is talent plus preparation. The problem with this view is that the closer psychologists look at the careers of the gifted, the smaller the role innate talent seems to play and the bigger the role preparation seems to play."

*Too-close paraphrase (still plagiarism)*:
> "Success seems to depend on a combination of talent and preparation. However, when psychologists closely examine the gifted and their careers, they discover that innate talent plays a much smaller role than preparation."

This fails the test — it's a near word-for-word substitution with the same sentence structure and order.

*Acceptable paraphrase*:
> "As Gladwell observes, summarizing studies on the highly successful, we tend to overestimate the role of talent and underestimate that of preparation."

This restructures the idea entirely rather than substituting synonyms into the original's frame — and notably does *not* put "talent" or "preparation" in quotes, since those are common enough words to use freely.

### Generic Phrases Are Not Plagiarism (Wallwork)
For non-native English writers especially, it's worth knowing explicitly: **generic connective and framing phrases can be reused freely from other papers** without citation, because they carry no unique content — they're the field's shared scaffolding, not intellectual property. Phrases like *"it has been suggested that,"* *"more generally, X has put forward a widely discussed position that,"* or *"it might be objected, though, that…"* are fair game to borrow as sentence templates. What must never be borrowed without citation is the *specific claim or finding* embedded inside that scaffolding. (This distinction is also a legitimate way to build English fluency — read papers in your field and note their generic transitional phrasing as reusable structure; see Chapter 19's "Useful Phrases.")

### Quoting, Paraphrasing, or Summarizing — Choosing Deliberately
Booth et al. frame this as an active choice, not a default:
- **Quote directly** when the source's exact wording is itself the evidence (e.g., analyzing an author's rhetoric, or when precise legal/technical wording changes meaning if reworded).
- **Paraphrase** when you can state the idea more clearly or more relevantly to your argument than the source does, in genuinely original phrasing.
- **Summarize** when you need the gist of a longer passage or argument, condensed and integrated into your own argument's flow.

## Key Concepts & Definitions

- **Plagiarism (functional definition):** presenting words, ideas, or methods that a source's original author would recognize as theirs, without proper attribution.
- **Common knowledge:** an idea widespread and unattached to a specific identifiable source within a given field — exempt from citation.
- **Patch writing:** stringing together lightly modified source material with minimal original synthesis (see Chapter 4) — a common gateway to unintentional plagiarism.
- **Generic phrase:** connective or framing language carrying no unique intellectual content, safely reusable without citation.

## Mental Models & Metaphors

- **Note-taking discipline as plagiarism insurance** — the root cause of most plagiarism is not intent but a failure, at the note-taking stage, to mark clearly what was copied vs. paraphrased vs. original thought (see Chapter 2). By the time a writer is drafting under deadline pressure, an ambiguous note is a landmine.
- **"Better to seem naive than dishonest"** — a useful tie-breaking heuristic whenever you're unsure whether a phrase needs quotation marks.

## Common Pitfalls / Anti-patterns

- **Believing publicly available web content doesn't need citation** because it's "free." It does.
- **Claiming innocent intent as a defense.** Booth et al.'s blunt framing: "we read words, not minds" — if the resemblance is close enough that the original author would recognize it, intent doesn't change the outcome.
- **Synonym-swapping paraphrase** — replacing individual words with synonyms while keeping the source's sentence structure and order intact. This is plagiarism even without a single verbatim phrase.
- **Losing track of a paraphrase's source** by the time you draft, because it wasn't clearly marked in your notes.
- **Over-citing common knowledge**, which clutters a paper and can (paradoxically) make genuine attributions harder for readers to spot.
- **Under-citing genuinely novel, source-specific ideas** because they "feel" well-known once you've absorbed them — a bias every experienced researcher has to consciously guard against.

## Key Takeaways

- Apply the "would they recognize it?" test whenever you're unsure if something needs a citation.
- Quote distinctive phrasing; don't bother quoting generic scaffolding language.
- Use the "look away, then write from memory, then check for synonym-matching" procedure for every paraphrase you're not fully confident in.
- Cite ideas tied to a specific, identifiable, not-yet-common-knowledge source — not established field-wide facts.
- Reusing another paper's *generic* connective phrasing is normal and useful; reusing its *specific claims* without attribution is not.
