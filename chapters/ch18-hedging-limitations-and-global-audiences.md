# Chapter 18: Hedging, Diplomacy, and Handling Limitations

**Primary sources:** *English for Writing Research Papers* (Wallwork), ch. 9; *Writing Science* (Schimel), ch. 18–19

## Core Idea

Every study has limitations, and every strong claim risks alienating a skeptical reviewer if stated with total certainty. Two related skills manage this: **hedging** (expressing claims with appropriate, honest caution rather than false certainty) and **limitation-framing** (disclosing a study's weaknesses in a way that builds credibility rather than undermining it). Both are about precision and honesty, not vagueness or weakness — a well-hedged claim is *more* accurate, not less confident.

## Frameworks Introduced

### Why and When to Hedge (Wallwork)
Hedging means **anticipating referees' and readers' possible objections** by not stating claims more assertively than your evidence actually supports. The term borrows from the literal hedge — a boundary fence offering protection — and functions the same way rhetorically: it protects a claim from being read as overreach.

**Hedging is not the same as vagueness.** You should still be maximally precise about what you found — hedging is about the *degree of certainty* attached to an interpretation, not about blurring the finding itself.

**When to hedge:** not every claim needs hedging — routine, well-supported statements ("Table 2 shows that X had higher values than Y") don't require it. Hedge specifically when making a claim that:
- Extends beyond what your specific data can fully establish (e.g., interpreting *why* a pattern occurred, not just *that* it occurred).
- Could reasonably be interpreted differently by another researcher.
- Contradicts existing consensus or "common knowledge" in the field.
- Claims priority or uniqueness ("this is the first study to...") that you can't fully verify against the entire literature.

**Worked example:**
> Overconfident: *"Our results demonstrate that students from humanistic fields produce longer written texts than students from pure sciences, and this is due to the fact that humanists are more verbose."*
> Hedged: *"Our results would seem to demonstrate that students from humanistic fields produce more written work than students from the pure sciences, and this may be due to the fact that humanists are generally more verbose."*

The hedged version doesn't weaken the finding — it accurately represents that the interpretation (verbosity as the *cause*) is one plausible reading among others, not an established fact.

### Concrete Hedging Techniques
1. **Add a qualifying phrase before the claim:** *"we believe," "as far as we know," "to the best of our knowledge."*
2. **Add a qualifying adjective or adverb:** *"possible," "generally," "apparently."*
3. **Replace absolute-certainty verbs with modal or tentative alternatives:** replace *prove, demonstrate, is* with *may be, would seem to, appears to.*

**Field and cultural variation:** hedging conventions vary significantly across research cultures — writers from some linguistic/scientific traditions write more assertively even in careful, honest work, and not every referee interprets an unhedged claim as arrogant. But because hedging is low-cost and rarely reduces a paper's chances of acceptance (and often increases them), it's a safe default when in doubt, especially for early-career researchers building credibility in an unfamiliar field's conventions. A now-famous example of extremely subtle, understated hedging: Watson and Crick's 1953 DNA structure paper phrased what was in fact a landmark discovery as merely "not escaping their notice" that their proposed structure "suggests a possible copying mechanism" — a masterclass in confident modesty that non-native or early-career writers shouldn't feel obligated to imitate stylistically, but whose underlying principle (claim exactly as much as the evidence supports, no more) is universal.

### Diplomatic Disagreement: Saving Face
When critiquing another author's work (a normal and expected part of the scholarly conversation — see Chapter 3's "creative disagreement"), diplomacy matters as much as substance:
- Frame a critique around the *findings* or *method*, not the *researcher personally* — critique what the data show, not the competence of who collected it.
- Soften direct criticism with hedged phrasing rather than blunt assertions of error.

### Disclosing Your Own Limitations Without Losing Credibility (Wallwork)
Disclosing your study's limitations **builds** credibility rather than undermining it — it signals honesty, professionalism, and enough expertise to correctly identify what could have gone wrong. The scholarly community needs this information both for ethical transparency and so others can learn from it. But limitations can be disclosed in ways that don't require taking full direct personal responsibility, using **impersonal grammatical constructions** that distance the writer from the flaw without hiding it:
- **Passive voice, no agent stated:** *"It was found that the containers for the samples had become contaminated."*
- **Adverbial framing:** *"Regrettably, the containers for the samples had become contaminated."*
- **Impersonal "it" constructions:** *"It is reasonable to hypothesize that..."; "It appears possible that..."*

These constructions let a writer disclose a real problem candidly while avoiding language that reads as a personal admission of incompetence — a legitimate rhetorical move, not a dishonest one, as long as the underlying disclosure is accurate and complete.

### "Yes, But" vs. "But, Yes" (Schimel)
A distinctive, high-leverage reframing of *where* in a paper to address limitations. The common instinct is **"yes, but"**: present results and conclusions, *then* discuss limitations at the end — often collapsing into the generic, weak "more research is needed" non-conclusion. The problem: putting "but" last makes the limitation the paper's final, most memorable message.

**Schimel's alternative: "but, yes."** Address limitations **as early as possible** — get them out of the way — then build toward a strong, undiluted conclusion that lands with full force, appropriately constrained by (but not undercut by) those limitations. This is not about hiding weaknesses — it's about sequencing: air the "dirty laundry" up front so readers process the rest of the story already knowing the constraints, rather than needing to mentally reprocess everything they just read once limitations are revealed at the end. Schimel argues this is not only better storytelling but **more honest** — it doesn't bury caveats where a skimming reader might miss them, the way fine print does.

**A related structural point:** many apparent "limitations" are actually a **mismatch between the question asked and the methods used to answer it** — not an inherent flaw in the data. The fix, when this is the case, is often to reframe the Introduction's stated question to match what the methods can actually establish, rather than trying to defend methods against a question they were never capable of answering. (Schimel's example: a method that can only detect ~1% of soil bacteria cannot claim to determine "the size of the bacterial population" — but it can honestly claim to characterize the subset of bacteria that grow under those specific culture conditions. Reframing the claimed scope, rather than overclaiming and then hedging later, solves the problem at the root.)

### Writing Global Science (Schimel)
For non-native English writers, a genuinely reassuring structural point: **story structure (OCAR, SCFL) is language-independent.** The hardest part of scientific writing — developing a clear, well-organized story — is not primarily an English-fluency problem; it's a structural-thinking problem that transcends language entirely. Structure comes *before* language in the SCFL sequence (Chapter 17) precisely because getting the story right matters more than, and is separable from, getting every sentence idiomatically polished. This doesn't eliminate the real, additional burden non-native writers face — but it reframes where the highest-leverage effort should go: get the structure and logic right first (a skill entirely accessible regardless of native language), and treat sentence-level English polish as a later, separate pass (where professional editing help, if available, is most efficiently applied).

## Key Concepts & Definitions

- **Hedging:** expressing a claim with a degree of certainty proportionate to the actual evidence, using qualifying language.
- **Face-saving constructions:** impersonal grammatical forms (passive voice, "it is..." phrasing) used to disclose limitations without framing them as a personal admission of failure.
- **"Yes, but" vs. "but, yes":** whether limitations are addressed after (weakening the ending) or before (strengthening the ending) the main conclusions.
- **Question-method mismatch:** a common root cause of apparent "limitations," often better solved by reframing the question's scope than by hedging around an overclaimed one.

## Mental Models & Metaphors

- **The hedge as a protective fence** — the etymological root of "hedging," reframing the technique as protection rather than weakness.
- **Limitations as "dirty laundry"** — better aired deliberately and early than left as fine print a reader might miss and then feel misled by.
- **Confidence and humility as a balance, not a spectrum extreme** — Schimel's framing that too much of either blinds a researcher, to limitations or to real accomplishments respectively.

## Common Pitfalls / Anti-patterns

- **Overclaiming certainty** on interpretive claims that go beyond what the specific data can establish.
- **Hedging routine, well-supported statements unnecessarily**, which dilutes prose and can read as uncertain about things that aren't actually in doubt.
- **Using "yes, but" structure**, leaving limitations as the paper's final, most memorable note.
- **Trying to defend methods against a question they can't actually answer**, instead of reframing the question's scope to match what the methods genuinely support.
- **Personalizing critiques of other researchers** rather than critiquing findings, methods, or interpretations.
- **Assuming English fluency is the primary obstacle to strong scientific writing** — story structure, which is language-independent, is often the higher-leverage place to focus.

## Key Takeaways

- Hedge claims that extend beyond your data, are open to alternate interpretation, or challenge existing consensus — but don't hedge routine, well-supported statements.
- Use qualifying phrases, softened adjectives/adverbs, or modal verbs to hedge, rather than vague or evasive language.
- Disclose your study's limitations candidly, using impersonal constructions if you want distance from direct personal blame — but never to obscure the disclosure itself.
- Address limitations early in a paper ("but, yes") rather than saving them for a weak closing "more research is needed" — this produces a stronger ending and, per Schimel, a more honest one.
- When methods and questions don't match, consider reframing the question's scope before assuming the answer is simply to add more hedges.
