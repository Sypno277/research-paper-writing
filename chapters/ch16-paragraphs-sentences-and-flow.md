# Chapter 16: Paragraphs, Sentences, and Flow

**Primary source:** *Writing Science* (Schimel), ch. 10–16

## Core Idea

The OCAR story logic (Chapter 15) operates at every scale — including the paragraph and the sentence. A paragraph is a "unit of composition" only when it tells a coherent short story with real internal structure, not just a string of sentences ended by an arbitrary line break. And the connective tissue between sentences — what makes prose "flow" rather than feel disjointed — is built by consistently linking each sentence's *topic* (opening) to information the reader already has, exactly the Booth/Williams "old before new" principle (Chapter 7), now developed into a full technique.

## Frameworks Introduced

### Point-First vs. Point-Last Paragraphs
Beyond the textbook "topic sentence + development" (TS-D) model, paragraphs can use any of the story structures from Chapter 15:
- **Point-first (TS-D / LD-style):** state the point, then develop it. Simple, clean, and should dominate most technical writing — a reader skimming just the first sentence of each paragraph should get the paper's essential argument.
- **Point-last (OCAR / LDR-style):** build toward a conclusion that arrives at the paragraph's end rather than its start. More demanding of the reader's patience, and should be used more sparingly — typically reserved for a paragraph making a genuinely surprising or climactic point you want to land with more force by delaying it.

**The paragraph-as-cell metaphor:** if words are atoms and sentences are molecules, paragraphs are cells — structurally cohesive units with internal organization and clear connective function to the larger organism (the paper). A paragraph is not a unit of composition merely because it ends with a line break where the writer needed to "come up for air."

### Managing Flow: Topic as Janus
The central technique for connecting sentences smoothly is managing each sentence's **topic** (its opening, what it's "about") so that it does two things simultaneously:
- **Backward-looking:** refers to information already familiar from the preceding text.
- **Forward-looking:** signals what the sentence itself will develop.

Schimel names this the **Janus function**, after the Roman god of doorways who faces both directions at once. When a sentence's topic successfully looks both backward (to what's known) and forward (to what's coming), readers experience the prose as flowing; when topics don't perform this dual function, prose feels disjointed even if each individual sentence is grammatically fine.

**Worked example:**
> Molecules are comprised of covalently bonded atoms. Molecules' reactions are controlled by the strength of the bonds. Molecules, however, sometimes react slower than bond strength would predict.

Here, each sentence's topic ("Molecules," "Molecules' reactions," "Molecules...") stays anchored to the same recurring subject, letting the reader track a single thread as it develops — a concrete instantiation of the old-before-new principle (Chapter 7) applied sentence-by-sentence across a paragraph.

### Two Levels of Flow
1. **Paragraph-level thematic coherence:** every sentence in a paragraph should share a coherent theme set by the opening. A paragraph jumping across eight unrelated topics (Schimel's cautionary example: a "stream-of-consciousness" paragraph about fisheries touching eight disconnected subtopics) reads as incoherent regardless of how well any single sentence is written.
2. **Sentence-to-sentence transitions:** the harder, more consequential skill — managing the handoff at each transition so readers don't "derail." Schimel's relay-race metaphor: sentences on the same team pass a baton at each transition, letting an idea flow cleanly from one sentence into the next rather than requiring the reader to reconstruct the connection themselves.

**Why transitions matter more than raw sentence quality:** losing a reader at a critical transition point can lose them permanently — for a paper or grant proposal, this literally means a rejection. Schimel's phrasing: the best-cited papers and best-funded proposals are "page turners"; a broken transition turns a page-turner into a "re-turner," with a rejection letter attached.

## Key Concepts & Definitions

- **Point-first vs. point-last paragraph:** whether the paragraph's main claim opens or closes the paragraph.
- **TS-D (Topic Sentence–Development):** the standard point-first paragraph model — a simplified case of the LD story structure (Chapter 15) applied at paragraph scale.
- **Janus function:** the requirement that a sentence's topic simultaneously reference familiar (backward) information and set up new (forward) content.
- **Thematic coherence:** the requirement that every sentence within one paragraph share a common topic/theme, set by the paragraph's opening.

## Mental Models & Metaphors

- **Words : sentences :: atoms : molecules :: sentences : paragraphs :: molecules : cells.** A layered scale metaphor emphasizing that structure and function compound at each level — a paragraph isn't just a longer sentence, any more than a cell is just a bigger molecule.
- **Janus, god of doorways** — the sentence-opening topic as a threshold simultaneously facing what came before and what comes next.
- **The relay-race baton pass** — flow as a series of successful handoffs between sentences, where the "baton" is the reader's attention and comprehension.

## Common Pitfalls / Anti-patterns

- **Treating a paragraph break as simply "where I ran out of breath"** rather than a deliberate structural unit with its own internal opening-development-resolution logic.
- **Overusing point-last paragraph structure**, demanding patience from readers who, in most technical contexts, want the point up front (TS-D should dominate; point-last should be the deliberate exception).
- **Writing paragraphs with multiple unrelated topics** stitched together without a shared theme — technically grammatical sentences that together produce an incoherent paragraph.
- **Opening sentences with genuinely new information** instead of a backward-referencing topic, breaking the Janus function and forcing the reader to work harder to place the sentence in context.
- **Assuming that individually well-written sentences guarantee good flow** — flow is a property of the *transitions between* sentences, not of sentences in isolation.

## Key Takeaways

- Choose point-first (TS-D) structure as your paragraph default; reserve point-last structure for moments where delaying the point genuinely earns more impact.
- Check every paragraph for thematic coherence: does every sentence's topic fit a single shared theme set by the opening?
- Check every sentence's opening for the Janus function: does it look backward to something familiar and forward to what's coming?
- Treat transitions, not individual sentences, as the primary site of "flow" — this is where readers most often derail.
- Remember that broken flow has real consequences: it can cost a paper its readers, and a proposal its funding.
