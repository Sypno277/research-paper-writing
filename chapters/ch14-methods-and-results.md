# Chapter 14: Methods and Results

**Primary source:** *English for Writing Research Papers* (Wallwork), ch. 15–16

## Core Idea

The Methods section has one overriding job: **give another researcher enough detail to replicate what you did**, in a logical (usually chronological) order, using past-tense passive constructions that put the procedure — not the researcher — in focus. The Results section has a different job: **report what you found, including what you didn't expect to find**, structured around your figures/tables rather than around a narrative of "then I did this."

## Frameworks Introduced

### The Methods Checklist
A well-built Methods section answers, as relevant to your field:
- What/who did you study, and what hypothesis were you testing?
- Where was the study conducted, and what characteristics of that location matter?
- How was the experiment/sampling designed, and what assumptions were made?
- What variable(s) were measured, and why?
- How were materials/subjects handled, housed, or treated — what precautions were taken?
- What equipment was used (including modifications), and where did it come from (vendor/source)?
- What protocol governed data collection?
- How was the data analyzed — statistical procedures, equations, software?
- What significance threshold was used?
- What references can stand in for a full re-description of a well-established technique?
- What difficulties were encountered?
- How does this methodology compare to, or advance beyond, previously reported methods?

**Provide enough quantitative detail** (concentration, temperature, weight, size, duration, etc.) for genuine replication — this is the section's core function, not a formality. **A common failure mode:** because you're intimately familiar with your own method, you may unconsciously omit steps that feel "obvious" to you but aren't obvious to a reader trying to reproduce the work. Deliberately check for this by imagining a colleague attempting your procedure using only your written description.

### Seven Ways to Open a Methods Section
Field convention varies, so check your target journal — but common openings include: (a) a general statement characterizing the method ("The method described here is simple, rapid, sensitive..."); (b) a reference to a prior paper's fuller description; (c) stating the source of materials; (d) describing how subjects/samples were selected; (e) naming the geographic/institutional setting; (f) directing readers to a figure showing the experimental setup; (g) starting directly with the first procedural step.

### Tense and Voice in Methods: Past Simple, Passive-Leaning
Methods sections are conventionally written in the **past simple tense**, predominantly in the **passive voice**. Two converging reasons:
1. **Temporal logic** — the actions described happened before the paper was written, and past tense also helps distinguish *your* actions (past simple) from established or ongoing knowledge in the field (often present simple).
2. **Focus logic** — the passive voice appropriately shifts emphasis from *who did it* to *what was done*, which is exactly what a Methods section should foreground. This is a case where the passive is **necessary and appropriate**, not a stylistic failure to be avoided — contradicting the blanket "avoid passive voice" advice some writers absorb from general style guides (see Chapter 6 and Chapter 7 for when passive genuinely helps vs. hurts).

A useful framing (attributed to biology educator Greg Anderson, generalizable beyond biology): the Methods section should read as though you were verbally narrating the experiment's conduct to a colleague — third-person, mostly passive, consistently past tense, and explicitly **not** written as a step-by-step imperative protocol (the way a lab manual instructs a reader to perform the experiment themselves).

### The Results Checklist
A well-structured Results section answers:
1. What did you find?
2. What did you *not* find?
3. What did you find that you weren't expecting — including results that contradict your hypothesis?

**Structuring principle:** organize the Results in the same order as the corresponding procedures appeared in the Methods, using figures and tables to sequence your answers to the three questions above.

### Opening the Results Section
Two common patterns:
- **General panorama first** — a brief orienting statement before the details ("Overall, the results presented below show that...", "The three key results of this empirical study are...").
- **Direct entry** — going straight to specific findings, often inviting the reader to a figure or table in the first sentence or shortly after ("Figure 1 shows the mass spectra obtained from...").

### Structuring the Body of Results (O'Connor's four-part sequence, endorsed by Wallwork)
1. **Highlight the results that directly answer your research question** (including relevant control results).
2. **Outline secondary results.**
3. **Give supporting information.**
4. **Mention any results that contradict your hypothesis, and explain why they might be anomalous** — critically, "not relevant to the hypothesis" is *not* the same as "contradicts the hypothesis"; the former can be excluded, the latter must be reported and addressed.

### Report Negative Results — Always
This is one of the chapter's firmest, most explicitly stated recommendations. A negative result (a hypothesis that wasn't supported) is not "wasted" research — it's genuine information (e.g., "the thing you were testing doesn't work"), and suppressing negative results distorts the broader scientific record (a documented, serious problem in fields like medicine, where selective non-publication of negative trial results has caused real harm). Two important caveats when interpreting your own negative results honestly:
- The negative result might mean your **hypothesis was wrong** and needs reformulating.
- It might instead reflect a **flawed experimental design or insufficient statistical power** — a different, more troubling possibility worth ruling out before concluding the hypothesis itself was wrong.

Don't assume results contrary to expectation are automatically "bad data" — if the work was carried out well, unexpected results are simply *your* results, requiring honest interpretation rather than dismissal. (Negative results are typically discussed more fully in the Discussion section — see Chapter 13.)

### Tense in Results
Like Methods, the Results section reports past events (what you found before writing the paper) and is therefore written in the **past simple**, typically in a mixture of active and passive constructions — with the choice between them following the same character/subject-familiarity logic covered in Chapter 6 and Chapter 7, rather than a fixed rule.

## Key Concepts & Definitions

- **Replicability standard:** the Methods section's core test — could another researcher, using only what's written, actually reproduce the procedure?
- **Negative result:** a finding that fails to support the hypothesis being tested — genuine data, not a null outcome to be hidden.
- **Relevance vs. contradiction (Results):** irrelevant results can be omitted; contradictory results must be reported and explained.

## Mental Models & Metaphors

- **Methods as narration, not instruction** — describing what *was done*, in the voice of someone recounting a completed procedure, not directing a reader through a protocol they're about to perform themselves.
- **Negative results as information, not failure** — reframing a "nothing happened" result as "I now know this thing doesn't work," which is itself a usable finding.

## Common Pitfalls / Anti-patterns

- **Omitting steps that feel obvious to the author** but are essential for a reader attempting replication.
- **Writing Methods as a step-by-step imperative manual** rather than a narrated past-tense account.
- **Applying a blanket "avoid the passive voice" rule to Methods**, where the passive is often the more appropriate choice.
- **Suppressing or downplaying negative or unexpected results** rather than reporting and honestly interpreting them.
- **Confusing "irrelevant to my hypothesis" with "contradicts my hypothesis"** when deciding what to include in Results — only the former is safe to omit.
- **Re-narrating the full Methods procedure inside the Results** rather than referring back to it and focusing on outcomes.

## Key Takeaways

- Write Methods with enough quantitative and procedural detail that another researcher could genuinely replicate your work.
- Default to past-simple, passive-leaning prose in Methods — this is a legitimate stylistic choice here, not a violation of good style.
- Structure Results around your figures/tables in the same order as your Methods procedures, not as a separate narrative.
- Always report negative and unexpected results, and interpret them honestly rather than dismissing them as failures.
- Distinguish "irrelevant" (can omit) from "contradictory" (must report and explain) when deciding what belongs in the Results section.
