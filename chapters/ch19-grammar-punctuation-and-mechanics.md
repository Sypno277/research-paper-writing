# Chapter 19: Grammar, Punctuation, and Mechanical Style

**Primary source:** *A Manual for Writers of Research Papers, Theses, and Dissertations* (Turabian), ch. 20–24

## Core Idea

Mechanical style — spelling, punctuation, number formatting, abbreviations — is the last thing to revise (see Chapter 5 and Chapter 17's top-down/SCFL sequences), but it still matters: readers judge a paper's care and credibility partly by whether these small, consistent conventions are followed correctly. The core principle across all of Turabian's mechanical guidance is **consistency**: pick a defensible rule where multiple options exist, and apply it uniformly throughout the paper, deferring to your discipline's or institution's specific requirements where they conflict with the general defaults below.

## Frameworks Introduced

### Spelling: General Principles
- Use American spelling conventions and a reliable dictionary (Turabian recommends Merriam-Webster) as your authority — except in direct quotations, where you preserve the source's original spelling exactly, even if it differs from your own paper's convention.
- Where a dictionary offers variant spellings separated by "or," pick one and use it consistently; where variants are separated by "also," default to the first-listed (preferred) form.
- If your discipline's conventional spelling differs from the dictionary's preferred form, follow your discipline's convention.
- **A spell-checker is not a substitute for careful proofreading** — it will not catch correctly-spelled-but-wrong words (*and* vs. *an*, *quite* vs. *quiet*), and it may not recognize proper nouns or discipline-specific terms.
- Always check whether your department, institution, or instructor has specific spelling requirements (particularly relevant for theses/dissertations) — these override general guidelines.

### Numbers: Words or Numerals?
Choose **one general rule** for your whole paper, based on your field and how numerically dense your writing is:
1. **Humanities/social sciences, occasional numerical data:** spell out numbers one through one hundred (hyphenate two-word numbers: *fifty-five*); also spell out round numbers followed by *hundred, thousand, million*, etc. Use numerals for everything else.
2. **Numerically dense writing (not full scientific data-reporting):** spell out only single-digit numbers; use numerals for everything else (10 and up).
3. **Sciences, numerically heavy:** use numerals for essentially all numbers, except at the start of a sentence.

**Special cases that override the general rule:**
- **Never begin a sentence with a numeral** — either spell it out or restructure the sentence.
- **Related numbers in a comparison** (some above, some below your threshold) should be expressed consistently as numerals across the whole comparison, even if your general rule would spell out the smaller ones: *"Of the group surveyed, 78 students had studied French and 142 had studied Spanish."*
- **Round numbers presented close together** should use numerals even under a spell-out rule: *"They sold 1,500 copies in the first year and 8,000 in the second."*
- Apply the same numerals-vs-words logic to **ordinal numbers** (*first, second* vs. *1st, 2nd*).

### Punctuation: Core Categories to Get Consistent
Turabian devotes detailed guidance to each punctuation mark; the practical takeaway is to establish a consistent house style for the marks most likely to vary across a long paper:
- **Commas** — separate clauses, phrases, and items in a series; get consistent about the Oxford (serial) comma before "and" in a list, since usage varies by field/style guide.
- **Semicolons** — join closely related independent clauses without a conjunction, or separate items in a complex list that itself contains commas.
- **Colons** — introduce a list, explanation, or elaboration that follows directly from what precedes it.
- **Hyphens vs. dashes** — hyphens join compound modifiers (*well-known example*); en-dashes and em-dashes serve different functions (ranges vs. parenthetical breaks) and are frequently confused — check your style guide's specific convention.
- **Quotation marks and multiple punctuation marks** — verify whether your style places closing punctuation inside or outside quotation marks (this differs between American and British convention) and stay consistent.

### Abbreviations: General Principles
- Distinguish between different **types** of abbreviations (acronyms, initialisms, contractions, symbols) since formatting conventions differ across types.
- Decide **when** abbreviation is appropriate — generally acceptable in citations, tables, and parenthetical material; generally spelled out in running prose on first use, especially for field-specific acronyms a general reader might not recognize.
- **Format consistently**: introduce an abbreviation by spelling it out in full on first use, followed by the abbreviation in parentheses, then use the abbreviation alone thereafter.
- Units of measure, personal names/titles, geographical terms, and time/date abbreviations each have their own specific conventions (e.g., when "Dr." or "Prof." is abbreviated vs. spelled out) — consult the relevant subsection of your style guide when in doubt, since these are genuinely field- and context-dependent rather than universal.

## Key Concepts & Definitions

- **House style / consistency principle:** the requirement to pick one defensible convention (spelling variant, number-formatting rule, punctuation style) and apply it uniformly throughout a single paper.
- **General rule vs. special case:** most mechanical style questions have a default rule (e.g., "spell out numbers 1–100") that gets overridden in specific, well-defined situations (numbers beginning a sentence, related-number comparisons).

## Mental Models & Metaphors

- **Mechanics as the paper's "grooming," not its substance** — this is genuinely the last layer of revision (see Chapters 5 and 17) precisely because it doesn't affect the argument's validity, but sloppy mechanics still cost a writer credibility with careful readers.

## Common Pitfalls / Anti-patterns

- **Mixing numeral conventions inconsistently** within one paper (spelling out "twelve" in one paragraph, writing "12" in the next, with no rule governing the difference).
- **Beginning a sentence with a numeral.**
- **Trusting a spell-checker to catch homophone errors** (*and/an, quite/quiet*) or discipline-specific terminology.
- **Inconsistent quotation-mark/punctuation placement** (mixing American and British conventions within one document).
- **Abbreviating a term in running prose without first spelling it out**, forcing readers to guess or backtrack.
- **Ignoring institution- or discipline-specific mechanical requirements** that override these general defaults (especially relevant for theses and dissertations with formal submission requirements — see Chapter 20).

## Key Takeaways

- Choose one numeral-formatting rule appropriate to your field and numerical density, and apply it consistently, respecting the documented special-case overrides.
- Never start a sentence with a numeral.
- Use a dictionary (not just a spell-checker) as your spelling authority, and preserve original spelling exactly within direct quotations.
- Establish consistent conventions for commas, semicolons, colons, hyphens/dashes, and quotation-mark placement before your final proofreading pass.
- Spell out abbreviations on first use, then abbreviate consistently thereafter — and always check institution-specific requirements for formal submissions.
