# Chapter 11: Tables, Figures, and Visual Evidence

**Primary sources:** *The Craft of Research*, ch. 15; *A Manual for Writers* (Turabian), ch. 8, 26

## Core Idea

Choosing between a table, a bar chart, or a line graph is not a cosmetic decision — each form has a **distinct rhetorical effect** on how readers interpret the same underlying data. Beyond choosing the right form, visual data presentation carries a genuine ethical obligation: the same numbers can be made to look dramatically different (or the same) depending on scale, framing, and chart type, and a writer is responsible for not exploiting that flexibility to mislead.

## Frameworks Introduced

### Terminology: Tables vs. Figures vs. Charts vs. Graphs
- **Graphics** (umbrella term): all visual representations of data.
- **Table:** a grid of rows and columns — precise, objective-looking, but requires the reader to infer trends themselves unless you state them explicitly nearby.
- **Figures:** everything else — charts, graphs, photographs, drawings, diagrams.
- **Charts:** figures built from discrete shapes (bars, circles, points) — emphasize *contrasts between discrete items*.
- **Graphs:** figures built from continuous lines — emphasize *continuous change over time*.

### Choosing the Right Graphic for Your Rhetorical Goal
The same underlying data (e.g., how family structure percentages shifted over four decades) can be presented as:
- A **table**, which reads as precise and neutral but leaves trend-spotting to the reader.
- A **bar chart**, which visually foregrounds discrete-period contrasts.
- A **line graph**, which visually foregrounds the sense of continuous, ongoing change.

**The choice should follow your rhetorical goal, not habit or software defaults.** If you want readers to register a trend over time, a line graph communicates that more viscerally than a table of the same numbers. If you want to emphasize precise, checkable values, a table is more appropriate — because it doesn't visually "argue" for an interpretation the way a chart does.

**Scope calibration:** beginners should generally stick to tables, bar charts, and line graphs. Advanced researchers in fields with established conventions for more complex visualizations (heat maps, scatter plots with regression lines, network diagrams) should follow those field conventions — but only once genuinely familiar with what each form communicates, not because default software offers them.

### Designing Graphics: Two Core Principles
1. **Frame each graphic to help readers understand it** — give it a clear title, labeled axes, and (where relevant) a caption or nearby sentence stating the point it supports. A well-designed graphic should be interpretable largely on its own, not require readers to reverse-engineer its meaning from your prose.
2. **Keep graphics as simple as their content allows.** Modern software makes it easy to add 3D effects, extraneous gridlines, decorative color gradients, and other visual noise. None of this helps readers — it usually hurts. Strip a graphic down to what's needed to make its point, and no further.

### Communicating Data Ethically: Four Guidelines
This is the chapter's most important and most concrete contribution — a direct, actionable ethics framework for visual data presentation:

1. **Do not manipulate a scale to magnify or reduce a contrast.** A vertical axis truncated to start at 80 instead of 0 turns a modest decline into a dramatic-looking cliff. The same data, presented on a full 0–100 scale, shows a much gentler slope. Both are "accurate" in the sense that the plotted points are correct — but one is designed to visually exaggerate a trend the raw numbers don't support to that degree.
2. **Do not use a figure whose visual form implies a relationship the data doesn't establish.** Plotting two different variables (e.g., union membership and unemployment) on two independent axes with different scales can make unrelated or weakly related trends *look* like they move together — visually implying causation or correlation the underlying numbers don't actually demonstrate.
3. **Do not make a graphic unnecessarily complex, or misleadingly simple.** Overly complex graphics obscure the point they're meant to support; overly simplified ones (e.g., stacking chart bands in an order that makes an unrelated band appear to rise when it isn't) can lead readers to misjudge which values are actually changing.
4. **If a table or figure supports a point, state that point explicitly nearby.** Never assume the visual "speaks for itself" — readers should not have to reverse-engineer your argument from a chart alone; a sentence stating what the graphic demonstrates should always accompany it.

**Worked example of visual distortion via stacking order:** two stacked-area charts can represent *identical* underlying data but create different impressions depending on which category is stacked at the bottom vs. the top. If a rapidly rising category is stacked at the bottom, the categories above it appear (visually) to rise too, even though their actual values are flat — readers misjudge which trend is actually driving the change. Placing the rising category at the top of the stack instead removes this illusion. This is a case where *no numbers are altered* and the chart is still technically accurate, yet the visual impression conveyed to a reader differs substantially by design choice alone — which is exactly why ethical presentation requires deliberate attention, not just numerical correctness.

## Key Concepts & Definitions

- **Graphics:** umbrella term for all visual data representations.
- **Table:** grid of rows/columns; precise but interpretively passive.
- **Chart vs. graph:** discrete-shape visuals (bars, points) vs. continuous-line visuals.
- **Truncated scale:** an axis that doesn't start at zero, which can visually exaggerate contrasts.
- **Dual-axis distortion:** plotting two variables on independently scaled axes in a way that implies a relationship the data doesn't support.

## Mental Models & Metaphors

- **A graphic as an argument, not just a display** — every chart or table makes a rhetorical claim about the data's shape, whether the writer intends it to or not. The only choice is whether that claim is made deliberately and honestly, or by accident (or worse, exploited).
- **Software defaults as a trap, not a guide** — the tools that make graphics dazzling by default are optimized for visual impressiveness, not communicative clarity; the writer has to actively resist the defaults.

## Common Pitfalls / Anti-patterns

- **Letting graphing software's default settings determine visual choices** rather than deliberately selecting the form (table/bar/line/other) that matches your rhetorical goal.
- **Truncating an axis** without a clear, disclosed reason, inflating the visual impact of a modest change.
- **Plotting two variables on independently scaled dual axes** in a way that implies correlation or causation the data doesn't actually establish.
- **Presenting a graphic with no accompanying sentence stating its point** — leaving readers to guess at the intended takeaway.
- **Overdecorating simple data** (3D effects, unnecessary gridlines, excessive color) that adds visual noise without adding clarity.

## Key Takeaways

- Choose table vs. bar chart vs. line graph based on the specific rhetorical effect you want (precision, discrete contrast, continuous trend) — not habit or software defaults.
- Frame every graphic with a clear title/caption and keep it as visually simple as the content allows.
- Never truncate a scale or use mismatched dual axes without disclosing why, and never let a chart imply a relationship your data doesn't support.
- Always state, in nearby prose, the specific point a table or figure is meant to demonstrate.
- Treat every visual design choice (not just numerical accuracy) as an ethical one — the same correct numbers can still mislead depending on presentation.
