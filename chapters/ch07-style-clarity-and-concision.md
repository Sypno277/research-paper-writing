# Chapter 7: Style — Clarity and Concision

**Primary sources:** *The Craft of Research*, ch. 17; *English for Writing Research Papers* (Wallwork), ch. 5; *A Manual for Writers* (Turabian), ch. 11

## Core Idea

Clarity is not a vague aesthetic preference — it is produced by specific, diagnosable sentence-level choices: which noun becomes the subject, whether actions are expressed as verbs or as smothered nouns ("nominalizations"), and whether each sentence opens with familiar or unfamiliar information. These three principles — **characters as subjects, actions as verbs, old-before-new** — explain most of what readers experience as "clear" or "dense" prose, and they can be diagnosed and revised mechanically, not just intuited.

## Frameworks Introduced

### Principle 1: Make Subjects Name Characters
A "character" (Booth et al.'s term) is whatever your sentences keep returning to as their agent — a person, a concrete thing, or (when the field demands it) an abstraction treated consistently as an actor. Compare:

> (a) *Locke frequently repeated himself because he did not trust the power of words to name things accurately.*
> (b) *The reason for Locke's frequent repetition lies in his distrust of the accuracy of the naming power of words.*

Version (a) is clearer because its subjects (*Locke, he*) are actual doers. In (b), the grammatical subject ("*The reason for Locke's frequent repetition*") is not a doer at all — Locke is buried inside a modifying phrase. **Diagnostic:** underline the first six or seven words of every clause. Are the subjects short, specific, concrete characters? If not, the sentence is a revision candidate.

### Principle 2: Express Actions as Verbs, Not Nominalizations
A **nominalization** is a verb or adjective turned into a noun (*decide → decision, precise → precision, resist → resistance*), typically ending in *-tion, -ness, -ment, -ence, -ity*. Nominalizing actions has a cascading cost: it strips the sentence of a specific verb, forces in extra articles and prepositions, and often displaces the real character into a modifier or drops them from the sentence entirely.

> (a) *Having standardized indices for measuring mood disorders, we now can quantify patients' responses to different treatments.*
> (b) *The standardization of indices for the measurement of mood disorders has now made possible the quantification of patient response as a function of treatment differences.*

Version (b) buries four real actions (*standardize, measure, quantify, respond*) inside abstract nouns, replaces them all with the single vague verb *made*, and turns "we" into "our" before dropping the actor altogether.

**Important caveat — this is not an absolute rule.** When a field's readers already treat certain abstractions as familiar characters (e.g., *measurements, treatments, hospitalization* to a medical audience), leaving them as nouns is fine — even preferable, since forcing every nominalization back into a verb can produce an awkward sentence about "citizens" or "you" that doesn't match the paper's actual subject matter. The real rule: **avoid stacking additional, unnecessary abstractions around a nominalization you do need.** A passage can use one or two abstract main characters (*democracy, institutionalization*) clearly, as long as it doesn't surround them with a further pile of nominalized modifiers.

**Diagnosis and revision procedure:**
1. Underline the first 6–7 words of each clause.
2. Test: are the subjects concrete characters (not abstractions)? Do the verbs name specific actions (not vague ones like *have, make, do, be*)?
3. If either test fails, find your intended characters (inventing them if necessary), find what they're doing, turn nominalized actions back into verbs, and rebuild the clause around characters-as-subjects and actions-as-verbs.

### Principle 3 (the most important): Old Before New
Readers follow a passage most easily when **each sentence opens with information they already have** (something mentioned in a previous sentence, or otherwise contextually familiar) and saves new, complex, or unpredictable information for the sentence's end.

> (a) *Because the naming power of words was distrusted by Locke, he repeated himself often. Seventeenth-century theories of language...had centered on this naming power. A new era in the study of language...begins with Locke's distrust.*
> (b) *Locke often repeated himself because he distrusted the naming power of words. This naming power had been central to seventeenth-century theories of language... Locke's distrust begins a new era in the study of language...*

Version (b) reads as more coherent — not because its sentences are individually simpler, but because each sentence after the first opens with something the reader already recognizes (*Locke; This naming power; Locke's distrust*), rather than unpredictable material (*the naming power of words; Seventeenth-century theories; A new era*).

**When old-before-new conflicts with characters-as-subjects, old-before-new wins.** This principle also resolves the active/passive debate: rather than following a blanket "avoid passive voice" rule (bad advice, followed mindlessly), ask instead whether the sentence opens with familiar information — if it does, whichever voice achieves that is the right choice.

**Diagnosis and revision:** underline the first 6–7 words of every sentence; check whether they're familiar to the reader (usually something used before); if not, revise so familiar information opens the sentence and new/complex information closes it.

### Wallwork's Concision Toolkit (complementary, more mechanical)
A set of concrete cutting techniques that reinforce Booth et al.'s principles at the word level:

- **Cut redundant modifiers.** "Small [in size], round [in shape], yellow [in color]" — the bracketed words add nothing once a concrete adjective is already doing the work.
- **Replace vague nouns with concrete ones**, or cut them outright. Words like *activity, factor, situation, process, phenomenon* rarely add value and should be tested for removal or replacement.
- **Prefer a single verb over a verb + noun construction.** *"Make a comparison of X and Y"* → *"compare X and Y."* Other common offenders: *achieve an improvement (improve), carry out a test (test), cause a cessation (stop), reach a conclusion (conclude), show an improvement (improve).* The verb+noun form also forces you to pick a "helper" verb (*do* or *make* a comparison?) — a decision the single-verb form sidesteps entirely.
- **Prefer verbs over nouns generally** — English leans on verbs more than many languages; overusing nouns produces heavy, static sentences. *"X was used in the calculation of Y"* → *"X was used to calculate Y."*

## Key Concepts & Definitions

- **Nominalization:** a verb or adjective converted into a noun (*resist → resistance*).
- **Character:** whatever a sentence's subject consistently refers back to as an actor across a passage — not necessarily a person.
- **Old-before-new:** the sequencing principle that sentences should open with familiar information and close with new information.
- **Verb + noun construction ("helper verb" pattern):** an inflated phrase like *"make an analysis"* that could be a single verb (*"analyze"*).

## Mental Models & Metaphors

- **Prose as a story about characters** — even a technical paper is, at the sentence level, "a story" about whatever entities keep recurring as subjects (proteins, institutions, variables, patients). Treating the writing this way — asking "who or what is the main character of this passage, and are they consistently the subject?" — is a surprisingly durable clarity heuristic.
- **The "first six or seven words" test** as a single repeatable diagnostic tool usable on any sentence, for both the character/subject principle and the old-before-new principle.

## Common Pitfalls / Anti-patterns

- **Blind rule-following on active/passive voice.** Neither voice is inherently clearer; the real test is whether the subject carries familiar information.
- **Nominalizing every action out of habit**, even when a field's readers would find the resulting noun-heavy sentence harder to parse than the equivalent verb-based one.
- **Stripping out abstractions your field's readers genuinely need** as established shared vocabulary, in an overzealous drive to "always use verbs."
- **Choosing the wrong helper verb** in verb+noun constructions (*do* vs. *make* a comparison) — a problem that disappears entirely once you use the single corresponding verb.
- **Padding sentences with redundant modifier pairs** ("fundamental and critical importance," "consistent and coherent manner") that add length without adding meaning.

## Worked Example: Full Revision Cycle

Original: *"The evaluation of this index has been carried out by means of the correlation function."*

Step 1 — find the buried action: *evaluation* is a nominalization of *evaluate*.
Step 2 — find the buried character: *this index* is the thing being acted on; the correlation function is the tool.
Step 3 — rebuild around verb + concrete subject: *"This index was evaluated using the correlation function."*

Result: shorter, more direct, and (per the old-before-new principle) still opens with the already-established subject (*this index*) if it follows a sentence that introduced the index.

## Key Takeaways

- Test every sentence's first 6–7 words for two things: is the subject a concrete character, and is it information the reader already has?
- Convert nominalizations back into verbs by default — but keep abstractions your specific readers already treat as familiar characters.
- Don't apply "avoid passive voice" as a rule; apply "open with familiar information" as the rule, and let voice follow from that.
- Replace verb+noun constructions with single verbs wherever a corresponding verb exists.
- Cut modifiers and vague nouns that repeat information already carried by a more concrete word nearby.
