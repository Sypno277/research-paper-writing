# Chapter 13: Introductions and Conclusions

**Primary sources:** *The Craft of Research*, ch. 16; *A Manual for Writers* (Turabian), ch. 10; *English for Writing Research Papers* (Wallwork), ch. 13, 17–18

## Core Idea

What seizes a reader's attention is not a "snappy" opening line — it's a **problem they believe needs a solution**, followed by a promise that you've found one. Across wildly different fields (cultural criticism, engineering reports, legal history), introductions share a single underlying structure — **Context + Problem + Response** — even though their surface style differs completely. Conclusions largely mirror this same structure in reverse.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The Common Structure of Introductions: Context, Problem, Response
1. **Contextualizing background** — establishes what readers already know or believe, creating a stable starting point.
2. **Statement of the problem** — disrupts that stable context by identifying a gap, contradiction, or unanswered question (echoing the "problem" concept from Chapter 1 and Chapter 3).
3. **Response to the problem** — either states your main point/solution directly, or promises that the paper will deliver one.

This pattern holds across radically different genres. A cultural-criticism essay might open with pop-culture context (android characters questioning what makes someone a "person"), pivot to a problem (do these characters reinforce narrow, culturally biased ideas of "normal"?), and respond (arguing that the archetype reflects exclusionary Western criteria). A corporate engineering report might open with a company initiative (interface redesign), state a problem (no data exists on which icons are self-explanatory), and respond (providing that missing data). The *content* is unrecognizable between the two — the *shape* is identical.

### Deciding Whether to State Consequences Explicitly
Once you've identified a problem (a gap in knowledge or a flaw in existing understanding), readers need to know **why the gap matters** — but whether you must spell that out explicitly depends on your audience:
- **State it explicitly** when readers might not already grasp why the problem matters, especially for a first research project, where you can't assume readers already understand the significance you're still discovering yourself.
- **You may omit it** when your audience already recognizes the stakes — Crick and Watson didn't need to explain why DNA's structure mattered to their intended readers; stating it would have seemed redundant or condescending.

**The So What? test applies here exactly as in Chapter 1** — after stating the gap/problem, ask "So what?" If you can't answer with a real cost (to knowledge, to practice, to understanding something bigger), the problem statement isn't yet convincing.

### Two Ways to Deliver the Response
1. **State the gist of your solution explicitly**, near the end of the introduction — most readers prefer this; it lets them evaluate relevance as they read and remember your point better.
2. **Promise a solution**, saving the actual claim for the conclusion — riskier, and requires (at minimum) a **launching sentence** naming the key terms/concepts the paper will develop, so readers aren't left directionless (see Chapter 4 for the same claim-placement decision from a planning perspective).

### Opening Techniques (use sparingly, in service of Context, not as decoration)
- **Open with a striking fact** directly relevant to your problem — not a generic attention-grabber unrelated to your actual argument.
- **Open with a striking quotation** that sets up the context or problem you'll address — again, relevance to the argument matters more than novelty for its own sake.

### Writing the Conclusion: The Introduction's Mirror
A conclusion can be built from the **same elements as the introduction, in reverse order**:
1. **Start with your main point** — if already stated in the introduction, restate it here more fully (never word-for-word identical — see Chapter 5's frame-revision check).
2. **Add new significance or application** — extend beyond mere summary by offering a fresh answer to *So what?*, pointing toward implications, applications, or new questions your research opens up. A strong conclusion is not just a recap; it's an opportunity to extend the conversation your paper joined.

### Wallwork's Practical Addition: Draft the Introduction Last
Consistent with the advice for abstracts (Chapter 12), experienced writers typically **draft the Introduction after finishing the body and results** — you can only accurately frame "why this matters" and "what gap this fills" once you know what you actually found. Drafting it first often produces a framing mismatch you'll have to fix later anyway.

### Discussion and Conclusion Sections in Scientific Papers (Wallwork)
In empirical science writing specifically, the Discussion section typically:
- Restates the key result(s) briefly (without repeating the full Results section).
- Interprets what the results mean, in light of existing literature.
- Explicitly compares findings to prior work — confirming, contradicting, or extending it.
- Addresses limitations honestly (see Chapter 18).
- The Conclusion (sometimes folded into the Discussion, sometimes separate per journal convention) then states the take-home message and, often, future directions.

## Key Concepts & Definitions

- **Context-Problem-Response (CPR) structure:** the near-universal three-part shape of introductions across fields.
- **Launching sentence:** the sentence closing an introduction that reserves the claim for the conclusion but still orients the reader with key terms.
- **So what? test (applied to introductions):** the check for whether a stated problem carries real stakes for the reader.

## Mental Models & Metaphors

- **Introduction as "disrupting a stable context"** — readers start in a comfortable state of assumed knowledge; the problem statement is what unsettles that comfort and creates the need the paper then satisfies.
- **"You can survive readers who disagree; you can't survive readers who don't care."** — Booth et al.'s framing of why establishing stakes matters more than being provocative.
- **Conclusion as introduction-in-reverse** — a genuinely useful compositional shortcut: if you know your introduction's structure, you already know your conclusion's skeleton.

## Common Pitfalls / Anti-patterns

- **Opening with a "snappy" or "cute" hook unconnected to the actual problem** — attention without relevance doesn't hold a reader.
- **Failing to state consequences when the audience genuinely needs them** (especially in early-career or interdisciplinary writing, where you can't assume shared background).
- **Over-explaining consequences to an expert audience that already shares your framing** — reads as condescending.
- **Repeating the introduction's point sentence word-for-word in the conclusion** rather than restating it more fully or specifically.
- **Treating the conclusion as pure summary**, missing the chance to answer *So what?* a second time with new significance.
- **Drafting the introduction first and never revisiting it** once the paper's actual findings and framing have evolved.

## Worked Example: Consequences Test

> *Statement:* "Motodyne has no data showing which icons are self-explanatory."
> *So what?* "Without such data, it cannot determine which icons to redesign."

This two-line pattern — state the gap, then answer *So what?* in the very next sentence — is a reliable template for testing (and building) a problem statement in any introduction.

## Key Takeaways

- Structure introductions as Context → Problem → Response, regardless of field or genre.
- Decide deliberately whether to state consequences explicitly, based on what your specific audience can be assumed to already know.
- Choose between stating your main point directly at the end of the introduction, or promising it (with a launching sentence) for the conclusion.
- Build your conclusion by reversing your introduction's structure: restate the point (more fully, not verbatim), then add new significance.
- Draft introductions (and abstracts) last, after you know what the paper actually found and argues.
