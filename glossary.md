# Glossary

**Acknowledgment & Response** — the argument element that anticipates and answers a skeptical reader's objections; demonstrates command of the intrinsic and extrinsic soundness of a claim. (Ch03)

**Action (OCAR)** — the story element describing what happens to address the Challenge; in a paper, the work done (Methods/Results). (Ch15)

**Audience patience continuum** — the spectrum of story structures (OCAR → ABDCE → LD → LDR) matched to how quickly a reader/reviewer needs the point. (Ch15)

**Backward vs. forward pronoun reference** — a pronoun should point to a noun already introduced (backward), never one still to come (forward). (Ch06)

**Bibliography** — the end-of-paper source list in Notes-Bibliography (Chicago) style; may include works consulted but not directly cited. (Ch09)

**Character (writing craft)** — whatever a sentence or passage's subject consistently refers back to as an actor; not necessarily a person — can be a molecule, institution, or concept. (Ch07, Ch15)

**Challenge (OCAR)** — the story element stating what the characters need to accomplish; the specific question a paper answers. (Ch15)

**Claim** — the central assertion a paper's argument supports; the thesis. (Ch03)

**Common knowledge** — an idea widespread and unattached to a specific identifiable source within a field; exempt from citation. (Ch08)

**Context-Problem-Response (CPR)** — the near-universal three-part structure of introductions: establish context, disrupt it with a problem, respond with a claim or a promise of one. (Ch13)

**Creative agreement / creative disagreement** — Booth et al.'s five-pattern technique for generating a research problem by extending or challenging a source's claims. (Ch02)

**Data dump** — a section with abundant evidence but no reasons explicitly connecting it to the claim. (Ch05)

**Declarative title** — a title phrased as a complete factual claim (subject-verb-object) rather than a topic description; risky if findings are tentative. (Ch12)

**Evidence** — the data behind a reason; distinct from a *report* of evidence, which is the writer's characterization of that data. (Ch03)

**Face-saving construction** — an impersonal grammatical form (passive voice, "it is..." phrasing) used to disclose a limitation without framing it as personal failure. (Ch18)

**Focused topic** — a topic narrowed using an action word (a noun derived from a verb) rather than left as a static noun phrase. (Ch01)

**Full note vs. shortened note** — in Chicago Notes-Bibliography style, the first (complete) citation of a source vs. later abbreviated citations of the same source. (Ch09)

**Generic phrase** — connective or framing language carrying no unique intellectual content, safely reusable across papers without citation. (Ch08)

**Graphics** — umbrella term for all visual data representations: tables, charts, and graphs. (Ch11)

**Hedging** — expressing a claim with a degree of certainty proportionate to the actual evidence, using qualifying language, to anticipate reviewer objections. (Ch18)

**Hourglass structure** — the shape of a well-built scientific paper: wide opening (broad problem), narrow middle (specific methods/results), wide resolution (broad implications). (Ch15)

**Janus function** — the requirement that a sentence's topic simultaneously reference familiar (backward) information and set up new (forward) content, enabling flow. (Ch16)

**Key-terms test** — Booth et al.'s diagnostic of circling a paper's recurring key terms to verify visible structural coherence. (Ch05)

**"Look away" paraphrase test** — reading a source, looking away, writing a paraphrase from memory, then checking for synonym-matching to detect too-close paraphrase. (Ch08)

**Nominalization** — a verb or adjective converted into a noun (e.g., *resist → resistance*); overuse buries actions and characters. (Ch07)

**OCAR** — Opening, Challenge, Action, Resolution: the four universal story elements underlying all scientific writing, at every scale. (Ch15)

**Old-before-new principle** — sentences should open with familiar information and close with new/complex information, for maximal reader comprehension. (Ch07)

**Parenthetical citation** — an in-text citation in parentheses (used in Chicago Author-Date and MLA styles) rather than a numbered note. (Ch09, Ch10)

**Patch writing** — assembling a paper primarily from strung-together quotations/paraphrases with minimal original analysis. (Ch04, Ch08)

**Plagiarism (functional definition)** — presenting words, ideas, or methods a source's author would recognize as their own, without proper attribution. (Ch08)

**Point-first vs. point-last paragraph** — whether a paragraph's main claim opens (TS-D structure) or closes (OCAR/LDR structure) the paragraph. (Ch16)

**Problem (research)** — a question whose non-answer costs the reader something they already care about; distinct from a merely curious question. (Ch01)

**Reader-responsibility vs. writer-responsibility** — whether the burden of connecting ideas across a text falls on the reader (many non-English traditions) or the writer (English scientific convention). (Ch05)

**Reference list** — the end-of-paper source list in Chicago Author-Date style; should generally include only sources actually cited. (Ch09)

**Reliability check (seven-point)** — Booth et al.'s checklist for evaluating a source: reputable press, peer review, reputable author, reputable sponsor, currency, presence of notes/bibliography, bibliographic metadata. (Ch02)

**Resolution (OCAR)** — the story element showing how characters/understanding have changed as a result of the Action; the conclusion. (Ch15)

**SCFL** — Structure, Clarity, Flow, Language: Schimel's prioritized, iterative revision-pass sequence, largest-scale issues first. (Ch17)

**Skim-to-evaluate-relevance** — a strategic pre-reading technique (index, abstract, intro/conclusion, headings) for judging a source's relevance before full reading. (Ch02)

**So what? test** — the three-step check (name topic → pose question → answer why it matters) for whether a research question has genuine stakes for a reader. (Ch01, Ch13)

**Storyboard** — a page-per-section sketch of a paper's argument structure, drafted before continuous prose. (Ch04)

**Thematic coherence** — the requirement that every sentence within a paragraph share a common topic/theme set by the paragraph's opening. (Ch16)

**Top-down revision** — revising in order: frame/structure → argument substance/quality → organization → paragraphs → sentences/mechanics; never the reverse. (Ch05, Ch17)

**Warrant** — the general principle licensing the inference from a reason to a claim; stated only when the reader won't follow the reasoning without it. (Ch03)

**Working hypothesis** — a tentative, provisional answer to a research question, used to organize early research and open to revision. (Ch01)

**"Yes, but" vs. "but, yes"** — whether a paper addresses limitations after its conclusions (weakening the ending) or before them (producing a stronger, more honest ending). (Ch18)
