# Cheatsheet

## Decision Rules

- **If** a topic is stated in 4–5 words with no action word, **then** it's too broad — rebuild it around a verb-derived noun (conflict, effect, contribution). (Ch01)
- **If** you can't answer "So what?" for a candidate question, **then** it's not yet a research problem — keep narrowing or reframing, or accept it's a personal-interest project only. (Ch01)
- **If** a source is tertiary (textbook, encyclopedia, Wikipedia), **then** use it for orientation only — never cite it as scholarly support. (Ch02)
- **If** a claim extends beyond what your data directly shows, is open to alternate interpretation, or contradicts consensus, **then** hedge it — otherwise don't bother. (Ch18)
- **If** a warrant states a principle your specific readers already accept, **then** omit it — stating it reads as condescending. **If** they might not accept it, **then** state it explicitly, before the reason it supports. (Ch03)
- **If** a section has evidence taking up less than ~1/3 of its content, **then** you likely don't have enough evidence for your reasons; **if** it's evidence-heavy with few reasons, **then** you likely have a data dump. (Ch05)
- **If** most paragraphs in a section don't contain any of your point-sentence's key terms, **then** the section is drifting — cut, rewrite, or work the terms back in. (Ch05)
- **If** a sentence's first 6–7 words are an abstraction or unfamiliar information, **then** revise: put a concrete character and familiar information first. (Ch07)
- **If** you can match your paraphrase to the source synonym-for-synonym in the same order, **then** it's plagiarism — rewrite from memory after looking away. (Ch08)
- **If** the original author would recognize your words/ideas as theirs, **then** cite — regardless of whether you quoted directly. (Ch08)
- **If** background/context in an abstract exceeds ~25% of its length, **then** cut it — readers want new information first. (Ch12)
- **If** you're tempted to end a paper with "more research is needed," **then** you're using "yes, but" — move the limitation discussion earlier and end on a real, appropriately-scoped conclusion instead ("but, yes"). (Ch18)
- **If** your Methods section reads like a step-by-step imperative manual, **then** rewrite it as past-tense narration of what was done. (Ch14)
- **If** a result contradicts your hypothesis, **then** you must report and explain it — only results *irrelevant* to the hypothesis can be omitted. (Ch14)
- **If** a chart's vertical axis doesn't start at zero without disclosed justification, **then** it's likely visually exaggerating a trend — reconsider the scale. (Ch11)
- **If** you're writing for a field/journal that permits "we," **then** use it for clarity; **if** it's prohibited (e.g., many physics journals), **then** use passive voice but make attribution unambiguous through tense and context. (Ch06, Ch14)
- **If** you find yourself narrating your research process ("First I did X, then I..."), **then** you're writing a process narrative, not an argument — restructure around claim/reasons/evidence instead. (Ch04)

## Revision Sequence (do not skip steps or reorder)

| Order | Booth et al. | Schimel (SCFL) | What you're checking |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Frame | Structure | Is the intro/conclusion boundary clear? Are OCAR elements present? |
| 2 | Argument substance/quality | (part of Structure) | Enough evidence? Reasons connected to claims? Warrants stated where needed? |
| 3 | Organization | Clarity | Key terms recur? Sections clearly bounded? Ideas concrete? |
| 4 | Paragraphs | Flow | Topic sentences? Janus function at transitions? |
| 5 | Sentences, spelling, punctuation | Language | Word choice, mechanics, final polish |

**Never revise bottom-up.** Polishing sentences before structure is settled wastes effort on material you may cut.

## Claim-Placement Decision

| Choice | Pros | Cons | When to use |
|---|---|---|---|
| State claim at end of introduction | Reader controls relevance-judging as they read; better recall | Feels less "dramatic" | Default — most readers prefer this |
| Reserve claim for conclusion | Can build suspense/drama | Risks losing or alienating readers; requires a launching sentence | Only deliberately, with clear signposting of key terms |

## Hedging Toolkit

| Technique | Example |
|---|---|
| Qualifying phrase before claim | "we believe," "to the best of our knowledge" |
| Modal/tentative verb | "would seem to," "may be" instead of "proves," "is" |
| Softening adjective/adverb | "possible," "generally" |
| Impersonal passive (for limitations) | "It was found that..." (no agent named) |
| Impersonal "it" construction | "It is reasonable to hypothesize that..." |

**Don't hedge:** routine, well-supported statements ("Table 2 shows X > Y").
**Do hedge:** interpretive claims, priority claims ("first study to..."), anything contestable.

## Citation Style Quick-Compare

| Feature | Chicago Notes-Bib | Chicago Author-Date | MLA |
|---|---|---|---|
| In-text marker | Superscript number | (Author Year, page) | (Author page) |
| Full source list | Bibliography (may include uncited works) | Reference list (cited only) | Works Cited (cited only) |
| Field | Humanities | Sciences/social sciences | Literature/languages |
| Element order (list) | Author, Title, Facts of pub. | Author, Date, Title, Facts of pub. | Author, Title, Publisher, Date |

**Rule: never mix styles within one paper.**

## Word-Order Priority List (English, non-native writers)

1. Subject before verb, always.
2. Subject and verb as close together as possible — no long parenthetical insertions between them.
3. Direct object immediately after its verb; indirect object after that.
4. Don't open with impersonal "it" — use a modal verb instead ("This may be due to..." not "It is possible that...").
5. Pronouns only point backward to an already-named noun, never forward.
6. Cap sentence length around 25 words for academic writing; split anything longer.

## Methods/Results Tense & Voice Defaults

| Section | Tense | Voice | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Methods | Past simple | Passive-leaning (necessary here) | Focus on what was done, not who did it |
| Results | Past simple | Mixed active/passive | Reports past findings; voice follows character/subject-familiarity logic |
| Introduction (established facts) | Present simple | Either | Distinguishes field knowledge from your own past-tense actions |
| Discussion (interpretation) | Present/past mixed | Either | Interpreting past results in present understanding |

## Visual Evidence Selection

| Goal | Best form |
|---|---|
| Precise, checkable exact values | Table |
| Emphasize contrast between discrete categories | Bar chart |
| Emphasize continuous change over time | Line graph |
| Beginner default | Table, bar chart, or line graph — stick to these three unless your field has strong conventions otherwise |

## Tells & Smells (fast diagnostic heuristics)

- **Topic in 4–5 static-noun words** → too broad, needs an action word.
- **Paper full of words like "activity," "factor," "process," "situation"** → concision problem, replace or cut.
- **Recurring "First I... then I... finally I..."** → process-narrative anti-pattern, not an argument.
- **Long sentence (60+ words) with 3+ unrelated ideas packed in** → split into multiple sentences.
- **Ending with "more research is needed" and nothing else** → weak "yes, but" structure; move limitations earlier.
- **Verb+noun construction ("make an analysis of," "carry out a comparison of")** → replace with single verb ("analyze," "compare").
- **Abstract/intro spending more than a few sentences on background** → cut toward the ~25% ceiling.
