# Patterns

## Three-Step "So What?" Sentence
**When to use**: Early, to test whether a candidate research question has genuine stakes; and again when drafting an introduction's problem statement.
**How**: Complete the frame — "I am studying ___, because I want to find out ___, in order to help my reader understand ___." If you can't fill in the third blank, the question isn't yet a "problem."
**Trade-offs**: Fast and diagnostic, but answering it honestly can be humbling early in a project — that's the point, not a flaw. (Ch01)

## Six-Angle Question Generation
**When to use**: When a topic feels too broad or you can't yet see a question in it.
**How**: Interrogate the topic's history, structure/composition, and categories — then invert each into negative/speculative form ("What if X never existed?").
**Trade-offs**: Produces many candidate questions quickly; still requires the So What? test to filter for genuine problems. (Ch01)

## Seven-Point Source Reliability Check
**When to use**: Before trusting or citing any source, especially on contested topics.
**How**: Check reputable press, peer review, reputable author, reputable sponsor (online), currency, presence of notes/bibliography (books), bibliographic metadata (websites).
**Trade-offs**: Thorough but time-costly if applied to every source; prioritize for sources central to your argument. (Ch02)

## Skim-to-Evaluate-Relevance
**When to use**: Before committing to a full read of a candidate source.
**How**: Book — index + first/last paragraphs of relevant chapters + prologue/summary. Article — abstract + intro/conclusion + headings. Website — same as article + About/site map.
**Trade-offs**: Saves substantial time; doubles as a check on your own paper's skimmability. (Ch02)

## Creative Agreement / Creative Disagreement
**When to use**: Reading a source specifically to generate a research problem, not just to gather facts.
**How**: Agreement — offer stronger evidence, confirm an unsupported claim, or extend a claim's scope. Disagreement — contradiction of kind, part-whole, developmental, cause-effect, or perspective.
**Trade-offs**: The perspective-contradiction pattern is most powerful (reframes the whole conversation) but hardest to execute well. (Ch02)

## Five-Element Argument Model
**When to use**: Structuring any research argument, from a single paragraph to a full paper.
**How**: Claim → Reasons → Evidence → Acknowledgment/Response → Warrant (stated only if needed).
**Trade-offs**: Build the core (Claim/Reasons/Evidence) first; add Acknowledgment/Response and Warrants after, to avoid analysis paralysis. (Ch03)

## Storyboard Planning
**When to use**: After research, before drafting continuous prose.
**How**: One page per section — Introduction (sources, gap, So What?, claim), one page per Reason (evidence, acknowledgment, warrant), Conclusion (restated claim, new significance).
**Trade-offs**: Cheap to revise before full sentences exist; reveals gaps (unsupported reasons, missing warrants) early. (Ch04)

## Three Flawed Plans Diagnostic
**When to use**: Reviewing a draft's overall organization.
**How**: Check for (1) narrative-of-your-thinking language ("First I... then I..."), (2) patchwork-of-quotations with minimal original analysis, (3) mirroring the assignment prompt's exact structure.
**Trade-offs**: Fast to check; fixing requires real restructuring, not just rewording. (Ch04)

## Top-Down Revision / SCFL
**When to use**: Any full revision pass on a complete draft.
**How**: Frame/Structure → Argument substance & quality → Organization → Paragraphs → Sentences/mechanics (Booth et al.); or Structure → Clarity → Flow → Language (Schimel) — functionally equivalent, largest-scale first.
**Trade-offs**: Passes are not fully separable in practice; expect to cycle through multiple times. (Ch05, Ch17)

## Key-Terms Circling Test
**When to use**: Checking whether a paper's organization is coherent.
**How**: Circle key terms in the main-point sentence (intro/conclusion), then circle their recurrences throughout the body. Paragraphs with none of the circled terms are candidates for cutting or rewriting.
**Trade-offs**: Concrete and repeatable; run it once for the whole paper and again per-section. (Ch05)

## Character/Verb/Old-New Sentence Diagnostic
**When to use**: Revising sentence-level clarity.
**How**: Underline the first 6–7 words of every clause. Test: is the subject a concrete character? Is the verb specific (not a nominalization)? Is the opening information familiar to the reader?
**Trade-offs**: Mechanical and repeatable, but requires judgment about which abstractions a specific audience already treats as familiar. (Ch07)

## "Look Away" Paraphrase Test
**When to use**: Producing any paraphrase of source material.
**How**: Read the source, look away, write the paraphrase from memory, then check for synonym-for-synonym matching in the same order as the original.
**Trade-offs**: Reliable for catching too-close paraphrase; doesn't replace the need to still cite the source. (Ch08)

## "Would They Recognize It?" Citation Test
**When to use**: Deciding whether something needs a citation.
**How**: Ask whether the original author, reading your paper, would recognize your words or ideas as theirs.
**Trade-offs**: Resolves most edge cases better than word-count rules of thumb. (Ch08)

## Four-Question Abstract Structure
**When to use**: Drafting or revising any abstract.
**How**: Why did you do this? What did you do/how? What did you find? What are the implications? Keep context-setting (question 1) under ~25% of total length.
**Trade-offs**: Adapt for fields without discrete "results" (e.g., history) rather than forcing the template. (Ch12)

## Context-Problem-Response (Introduction Structure)
**When to use**: Drafting or revising any introduction.
**How**: Establish context → state the problem/gap → respond (state the claim, or promise one with a launching sentence).
**Trade-offs**: Universal across fields in Booth et al.'s analysis; choice of stating vs. promising the claim changes reader experience significantly. (Ch13)

## Consequences ("So What?") Test for Problem Statements
**When to use**: After stating a research gap in an introduction.
**How**: Follow the gap statement with an explicit "So what?" and answer it in the next sentence.
**Trade-offs**: Skip explicit statement only when the audience demonstrably already shares the stakes (risk of condescension otherwise). (Ch13)

## OCAR Story Mapping onto IMRaD
**When to use**: Planning or auditing a paper's overall structure.
**How**: Map Opening+Background+Challenge → Introduction; Action → Methods+Results; Resolution → Discussion/Conclusion. Check the paper forms an hourglass (wide-narrow-wide).
**Trade-offs**: IMRaD is a rule (varies by field); OCAR is the underlying principle that must hold regardless. (Ch15)

## Janus Topic-Sentence Check
**When to use**: Diagnosing why a paragraph or passage feels disjointed despite grammatically correct sentences.
**How**: Check whether each sentence's opening (topic) both refers back to familiar information and sets up what's coming.
**Trade-offs**: The primary lever for paragraph-to-paragraph "flow," distinct from within-paragraph thematic coherence. (Ch16)

## "But, Yes" Limitation Sequencing
**When to use**: Deciding where in a paper/section to address limitations.
**How**: Address limitations early (not as a final "yes, but... more research is needed"), then build to a strong, appropriately-constrained conclusion.
**Trade-offs**: Requires more courage up front but produces a stronger, more honest ending. (Ch18)

## Question-Method Realignment
**When to use**: When a "limitation" turns out to be a mismatch between what was asked and what the method can answer.
**How**: Reframe the introduction's stated question/scope to match what the method actually establishes, rather than overclaiming and hedging later.
**Trade-offs**: Solves the root cause rather than patching symptoms with more hedges. (Ch18)

## Visual Data Ethics Checklist
**When to use**: Designing any table, chart, or graph.
**How**: Don't manipulate scale to magnify/reduce contrast; don't imply relationships the data doesn't support (e.g., mismatched dual axes); don't over- or under-simplify; always state the point a graphic supports in nearby prose.
**Trade-offs**: None of these constrain honest visualization — they only rule out manipulative defaults. (Ch11)
